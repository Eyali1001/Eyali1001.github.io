Chapter 2 — Models: putting a probability distribution on labelings

Take the 2×22\times2 grid wrapped around in both directions, so that it becomes a torus as in chapter 1. It has 4 vertices, 8 edges and 4 faces. Flip a fair coin for every edge and write +1+1 or −1-1 on it. How many faces do you expect to be defects, meaning faces whose face product is −1-1? Now suppose someone tells you that defects are expensive, and that a labeling with many of them should be less likely. How would you tilt the coins?

This chapter answers that question, and the answer is the central object of the whole course. We stop looking at one labeling and start looking at a probability distribution over all labelings. We call that distribution a model. Everything that comes later (averages, correlations, and in chapter 5 even "dynamics") will be read off from the model alone.

What you'll learn

  • What it means to pick a group element "uniformly at random", and why that needs a compact group.
  • How a cost on faces and a single number β\beta (the stiffness) turn the uniform distribution into a model.
  • Why the redundancy from chapter 1 (reframings) does no harm, and why, on a planar grid with the coin group, the faces are independent coins.

Prerequisites: chapter 1 (boards, labelings, face products, reframings, classes, tree normal form). Chapter 0 helps for intuition but is not required.

1. Choosing a label "uniformly at random"

Before we can make labelings unequally likely, we need to know what equally likely means. For a fair coin this is obvious: +1+1 and −1-1 each get probability 12\tfrac12. For a general group the right notion is this: picking an element at random and then multiplying it by any fixed element should not change the odds.

Definition (uniform distribution, glossary 4.1). Let GG be a group. A uniform distribution on GG is a probability distribution σ\sigma on GG such that, for every fixed g∈Gg\in G and every set A⊆GA\subseteq G,

σ(gA)=σ(A),gA={ga:a∈A}. \sigma(gA)=\sigma(A),\qquad gA=\lbrace ga : a\in A\rbrace .

In words: if xx is drawn from σ\sigma, then gxgx is drawn from σ\sigma too.

Our examples:

  • Coin group ℤ2={+1,−1}\mathbb Z_2=\lbrace +1,-1\rbrace: each element gets probability 12\tfrac12.
  • ℤN\mathbb Z_N (integers mod NN): each element gets probability 1N\tfrac1N.
  • Circle U(1)U(1) (angles mod 2π2\pi): the angle is uniform on [0,2π)[0,2\pi).
  • Rotations SO(3)SO(3): a "uniformly random rotation". As we'll see in a moment, this is not what you might first guess.

Fact 2.1 (when a uniform distribution exists). A uniform distribution on GG exists exactly when GG is compact, and then it is unique. For us, "compact" means one of two things: finite (like ℤ2\mathbb Z_2, ℤN\mathbb Z_N, the permutations S3S_3), or a closed and bounded set of matrices (like the circle or the rotations SO(3)SO(3)).

The exchange rates of chapter 0 live in ℝ>0\mathbb R_{>0}, which is not compact. Picking a "uniformly random positive number" is as impossible as picking a uniformly random integer. That is why chapter 0 had no randomness in it.

A surprise: a uniform rotation does not have a uniform angle

Every rotation of 3D space turns by some angle ω∈[0,π]\omega\in[0,\pi] about some axis. You might expect a uniformly random rotation to have a uniformly random angle. It doesn't.

Fact 2.2 (angle of a uniform rotation). If RR is drawn from the uniform distribution on SO(3)SO(3), its rotation angle ω∈[0,π]\omega\in[0,\pi] has density p(ω)=(1−cos⁡ω)/πp(\omega)=(1-\cos\omega)/\pi.

Why, roughly: the rotations by a fixed angle ω\omega form a whole sphere's worth of axes, and that sphere gets bigger as ω\omega grows. Large angles have "more room", so tiny rotations are rare.

The figure checks two things. The filled histogram is 200,000200{,}000 uniform rotations; look at how it follows the dark curve and not the flat dashed line. The outlined histogram is the same rotations each multiplied by one fixed rotation gg. It is indistinguishable from the first, which is exactly the defining property of "uniform".

Recap. A uniform distribution σ\sigma on a group GG is one that multiplying by a fixed element leaves unchanged. It exists (and is unique) exactly for compact groups: finite groups, the circle, rotations. It does not exist for ℝ>0\mathbb R_{>0}. "Uniform" is a statement about the group, not about any coordinate, so a uniform rotation does not have a uniform angle.

  1. Why can't there be a uniform distribution on ℝ>0\mathbb R_{>0} (with multiplication)?
Answer

Invariance under multiplying by 2 would force the intervals [1,2),[2,4),[4,8),…[1,2),[2,4),[4,8),\dots (and [12,1),…[\tfrac12,1),\dots) to all have the same probability. 2. Putting a price on defects: face cost and total cost

Now the tilt. We want labelings with defects to be less likely, and big defects even less likely than small ones. So we first attach a price to every face.

Definition (face cost, glossary 4.2). A face cost is a function φ:G→ℝ\varphi:G\to\mathbb R that is a class function, so φ(hxh−1)=φ(x)\varphi(hxh^{-1})=\varphi(x), and that is smallest at the identity. We use the standard choice, written in lgt as standard_face_cost(G):

group face cost φ(x)\varphi(x)
coin ℤ2\mathbb Z_2 φ(+1)=0,φ(−1)=2\varphi(+1)=0,\ \varphi(-1)=2
circle U(1)U(1), and ℤN\mathbb Z_N drawn as angles 1−cos⁡θ1-\cos\theta
matrices (S3S_3 as permutation matrices, SO(3)SO(3)) Re⁡tr⁡(I−x)\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(I-x)

Definition (total cost, glossary 4.3). For a board XX with faces FF, the total cost of a labeling UU is

S(U)=∑f∈Fφ(Uf)∈ℝ≥0,S:GE→ℝ≥0. S(U)=\sum_{f\in F}\varphi(U_f)\ \in\mathbb R_{\ge0},\qquad S:G^E\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}.

For the coin group, S(U)=2×(number of defects)S(U)=2\times(\text{number of defects}).

The plot shows the face cost on the circle, with the elements of ℤ2\mathbb Z_2 and ℤ5\mathbb Z_5 sitting on the same curve. ℤN\mathbb Z_N is a finite "pixelation" of the circle.

Why do we insist that φ\varphi is a class function? A face product depends on where you start walking around the face, and on the reframing. Both change it only by a conjugation (glossary 3.5). A class function doesn't notice either.

Fact 2.3 (total cost is an invariant). For every labeling UU and every reframing h:V→Gh:V\to G, S(reframed U)=S(U)S(\text{reframed }U)=S(U).

3. Stiffness and the model

One more ingredient: a single number that says how seriously to take the cost.

Definition (stiffness, glossary 4.4). The stiffness β∈ℝ≥0\beta\in\mathbb R_{\ge0} is a number. Large β\beta means defects are heavily punished; β=0\beta=0 means cost is ignored.

Definition (model, glossary 4.5). Let σE\sigma^E be "every edge label uniform and independent", a probability distribution on GEG^E. The model with board XX, group GG, face cost φ\varphi and stiffness β\beta is the probability distribution μβ\mu_\beta on GEG^E given by

μβ(dU)=1Z(β)e−βS(U)σE(dU),Z(β)=∫e−βS(U)σE(dU)∈ℝ>0. \mu_\beta(dU)=\frac{1}{Z(\beta)} e^{-\beta S(U)} \sigma^E(dU),\qquad Z(\beta)=\int e^{-\beta S(U)} \sigma^E(dU)\ \in\mathbb R_{>0}.

For a finite group, the integral is just an average over the |G||E||G|^{|E|} labelings, and this reads

μβ(U)=e−βS(U)∑U′e−βS(U′). \mu_\beta(U)=\frac{e^{-\beta S(U)}}{\sum_{U'} e^{-\beta S(U')}}.

Z(β)Z(\beta) is just the normalizing number that makes the probabilities add up to 1.

The stance of this course. The model μβ\mu_\beta is the object we study. The cost is not extra information on top of it. Up to the constant log⁡Z\log Z, the scaled cost βS\beta S is just minus the logarithm of the density of μβ\mu_\beta relative to uniform:

−log⁡μβ(U)σE(U)=βS(U)+log⁡Z(β). -\log\frac{\mu_\beta(U)}{\sigma^E(U)}=\beta S(U)+\log Z(\beta).

So a model is completely specified by four things: (board, GG, φ\varphi, β\beta).

For finite groups, lgt computes μβ\mu_\beta exactly, by listing every labeling. Let's do that for the coin model on the 2×22\times2 torus from the opening puzzle: 28=2562^8=256 labelings.

Up to sign, scale and a constant, the cost is the log-density relative to uniform, so the model is the single object that holds everything.

4. Redundancy does no harm

In chapter 1 we saw that a labeling carries both real information (its class) and convention (a reframing). Does that convention distort the model? No, for three reasons.

Fact 2.4 (the model doesn't see reframings). For every reframing h:V→Gh:V\to G and every labeling UU: μβ(reframed U)=μβ(U)\mu_\beta(\text{reframed }U)=\mu_\beta(U). Equivalently, reframing a random labeling drawn from μβ\mu_\beta gives a random labeling with the same distribution μβ\mu_\beta.

Why: a reframing permutes the labelings, it leaves σE\sigma^E unchanged, and it leaves SS unchanged (Fact 2.3). Below we reframe all 256 labelings at once, see where each one lands, and compare probabilities.

def index_of(U):
    """Position of a coin labeling in the enumeration order of `Model.exact()`."""
    bits = (np.asarray(U) == -1).astype(np.int64)
    return bits @ (2 ** np.arange(bits.shape[-1])[::-1])

assert np.all(index_of(ex.U) == np.arange(len(ex.U)))
h = random_reframing(Z2(), T, rng)
moved = index_of(reframe(Z2(), T, ex.U, h))           # where each labeling goes
print("reframing permutes the labelings:", len(np.unique(moved)) == len(moved))
print("and preserves every probability: ", np.allclose(ex.p[moved], ex.p))

Next, how big are the classes? For an abelian group they all have the same size.

Fact 2.5 (class sizes). If GG is finite and abelian (recall that boards are connected), every class contains exactly |G||V|−1|G|^{|V|-1} labelings, and there are |G||E|−|V|+1|G|^{|E|-|V|+1} classes. Hence the probability of a class is proportional to e−βSe^{-\beta S} of any of its members.

For non-abelian GG, classes can have different sizes.

Why the exponent is |V|−1|V|-1: there are |G||V||G|^{|V|} reframings. When GG is abelian, a reframing leaves a labeling unchanged exactly when h(s)=h(t)h(s)=h(t) on every edge, that is, when h≡gh\equiv g is constant. So each labeling in a class is reached by exactly |G||G| reframings, and the class has |G||V|/|G||G|^{|V|}/|G| members.

The most useful form of "redundancy does no harm" is the next fact. It lets us throw the redundancy away before computing. It is about averages: for a function f:GE→ℝf:G^E\to\mathbb R, we write ⟨f⟩β∈ℝ\langle f\rangle_\beta\in\mathbb R for its expectation when UU is drawn from μβ\mu_\beta (glossary 4.6). Chapter 3 is all about averages; here we only need the notation.

Fact 2.6 (fixing the tree is free). Pick a spanning tree. For any invariant f:GE→ℝf:G^E\to\mathbb R, the average ⟨f⟩β\langle f\rangle_\beta equals the average of ff in the smaller model where every tree edge is fixed to the identity and only the |E|−|V|+1|E|-|V|+1 other labels are random (with the same weight e−βSe^{-\beta S}).

Why: putting a uniformly random labeling into tree normal form leaves the non-tree labels uniform and independent. Each of them is a walk product that contains its own edge's uniform label exactly once. Invariants and SS don't change.

Let's verify this with two invariants on the torus (the number of defects, and the face product of one face) and one on the S3S_3 triangle (χ\chi of the loop product, where χ\chi is the trace of the permutation matrix).

def tree_fixed_average(model, f):
    """Average of f when tree labels are pinned to the identity (Fact 2.6)."""
    tree = model.board.spanning_tree()[0]
    ex = model.exact()
    keep = np.all(model.G.is_identity(ex.U[:, tree]), axis=1)
    p = ex.p[keep] / ex.p[keep].sum()
    return float(np.sum(p * f(ex.U[keep]))), int(keep.sum())

M = Model(T, Z2(), beta=0.7)

5. The stiffness dial

What does turning β\beta do? Look at the two ends first.

Fact 2.7 (the two ends of the dial).

  • At β=0\beta=0, μ0=σE\mu_0=\sigma^E: all labels are independent and uniform.
  • As β→∞\beta\to\infty, μβ\mu_\beta piles up on the labelings with no defects (cost 0), spread uniformly over them.

On a torus, "no defects" is a weaker condition than "consistent" (glossary 2.6). A loop that goes once around the torus is not a product of faces, so its loop product can still be −1-1 when every face product is +1+1. On the 2×22\times2 torus there are 32 defect-free labelings, but only 8 consistent ones.

At large β\beta, P(consistent)P(\text{consistent}) settles at 8/32=148/32=\tfrac14: the model spreads evenly over the 32 defect-free labelings, and only 8 of them are consistent.

Now the whole dial, on the 3×33\times3 torus (218=262,1442^{18}=262{,}144 labelings, still exact). The weight of a labeling depends only on its number of defects kk. So we count the labelings with each kk once, at β=0\beta=0, and reweight: Pβ(k)∝Nke−2βkP_\beta(k)\propto N_k e^{-2\beta k}, where NkN_k is that count.

What to look at:

  • Left: at β=0\beta=0 the defect count is a binomial-like hump around half the faces (only even counts are possible, as before). As β\beta grows the hump slides to 0.
  • Right: the average fraction of defect faces drops smoothly. The dashed curve is a formula for planar grids (not wrapped around), coming next.

On a planar grid, the faces are independent coins

The dashed curve comes from a fact that makes the coin model on a planar grid almost embarrassingly simple. Chapter 3 builds on it.

Fact 2.8 (independent faces). In the coin model (G=ℤ2G=\mathbb Z_2, standard face cost) on a planar grid (not wrapped around), the face products Uf∈{±1}U_f\in\lbrace\pm1\rbrace, one per face ff, are independent. Each is a defect with probability P(Uf=−1)=e−2β/(1+e−2β)P(U_f=-1)=e^{-2\beta}/(1+e^{-2\beta}); equivalently, ⟨Uf⟩β=tanh⁡β\langle U_f\rangle_\beta=\tanh\beta.

Why, lightly: put the labeling in tree normal form (Fact 2.6). On a planar grid the number of non-tree edges equals the number of faces, since |E|−|V|+1=(L−1)2|E|-|V|+1=(L-1)^2 for an L×LL\times L grid. The non-tree labels and the face products determine each other one-to-one. So the face products are independent fair coins under σE\sigma^E. The weight e−βS=∏fe−βφ(Uf)e^{-\beta S}=\prod_f e^{-\beta\varphi(U_f)} then tilts each coin separately.

Let's check it exactly on the 3×33\times3 grid (12 edges, 4 faces). We compare all 16 joint probabilities of the four face products with the product of the four marginals.

P3 = Board.grid(3, 3)
beta = 0.6
exP = Model(P3, Z2(), beta=beta).exact()

patterns = np.array(np.meshgrid(*[[1, -1]] * 4, indexing="ij")).reshape(4, -1).T
marg = (1 + np.tanh(beta) * patterns) / 2                # P(U_f = s) = (1 + s tanh beta)/2
joint = np.array([exP.p[np.all(FP == s, axis=1)].sum() for s in patterns])

      f"{np.abs(joint - marg.prod(axis=1)).max():.1e}")
print(f"<U_f> = {exP.average(lambda U: face_products(Z2(), P3, U)[:, 0]):.5f}"
      f"   tanh(beta) = {np.tanh(beta):.5f}")

The independence fails on a torus, and we already know why: there the product of all face products is forced to be +1+1.

Seeing the dial

Fact 2.8 hands us an exact sampler for large planar grids, far too large to enumerate. Flip one tilted coin per face. Then build any labeling with those face products: keep vertical edges at +1+1 and fill in the horizontal edges row by row. Finally apply a random reframing so that the labeling doesn't look special. (By Fact 2.4 the result is an exact draw from μβ\mu_\beta.)

What to look at:

  • The faces: the coral defects thin out as β\beta grows.
  • The edges: the gold −1-1 edges look like the same random noise at every β\beta.

Edge labels are mostly convention. The information lives on the faces.

def sample_coin_planar(L, beta, rng):
    """Exact draw from the coin model on an L x L planar grid (Fact 2.8 + Fact 2.4)."""
    B, G = Board.grid(L, L), Z2()
    idx = B.meta["edge_index"]                           # (vertex, direction) -> edge
    p = np.exp(-2 * beta) / (1 + np.exp(-2 * beta))
    face = np.where(rng.random((L - 1, L - 1)) < p, -1, 1)
    U = G.identity(B.n_edges)                            # vertical edges stay +1
    for y in range(L - 1):
        for x in range(L - 1):                           # face (x,y) = bottom edge * top edge
            U[idx[(x + L * (y + 1), 0)]] = U[idx[(x + L * y, 0)]] * face[x, y]
    return B, reframe(G, B, U, random_reframing(G, B, rng))
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(16, 4.4))
for ax, beta in zip(axs, [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]):
    B, U = sample_coin_planar(10, beta, rng)
    viz.draw_board(ax, B, U, Z2(), show_arrows=False, vertex_size=8, pad=0.2,
                   title=fr"$\beta={beta}$:  {defects(Z2(), B, U).sum()} defects of {B.n_faces}")
plt.show()

Recap. At β=0\beta=0 the model is uniform, and as β→∞\beta\to\infty it piles up on the defect-free labelings. On a torus those include non-consistent ones, whose loop product once around the torus is not the identity. On a planar grid with the coin group, the faces are independent coins with ⟨Uf⟩β=tanh⁡β\langle U_f\rangle_\beta=\tanh\beta (Fact 2.8). Pictures of samples show the lesson of this chapter: edges look like noise, and faces carry the information.

Check yourself

  1. In the planar-grid coin model, what is the probability that a given face is a defect at β=0\beta=0? At β=12ln⁡3\beta=\tfrac12\ln 3?
Answer

At β=0\beta=0 it is 12\tfrac12. At β=12ln⁡3\beta=\tfrac12\ln3, e−2β=13e^{-2\beta}=\tfrac13, so the probability is 1/31+1/3=14\frac{1/3}{1+1/3}=\tfrac14.

  1. Why can a torus never have exactly one defect?
Answer

Every edge lies on exactly two faces. In the product of all face products, each label appears twice, and (±1)2=1(\pm1)^2=1. So the product of all face products is +1+1, which means the number of defects is even.

  1. The 10×1010\times10 grid in the dial picture has 180180 edges. How many labelings is that, and why could we still sample it exactly?
Answer

2180≈1.5×10542^{180}\approx1.5\times10^{54} labelings, far beyond enumeration. Fact 2.8 reduces the problem to 81 independent tilted coins, one per face. Fact 2.4 lets us reframe the result freely.

Chapter recap

  • A uniform distribution on GG is unchanged by multiplying by a fixed element. It exists exactly for compact GG (finite groups, circle, rotations), not for ℝ>0\mathbb R_{>0}. A uniform rotation's angle has density (1−cos⁡ω)/π(1-\cos\omega)/\pi.
  • A face cost φ:G→ℝ\varphi:G\to\mathbb R is a class function, minimal at the identity. The total cost S(U)=∑fφ(Uf)S(U)=\sum_f\varphi(U_f) is an invariant.
  • The model μβ∝e−βSσE\mu_\beta\propto e^{-\beta S} \sigma^E is determined by (board, GG, φ\varphi, β\beta). The cost is its log-density relative to uniform, so the model is the one object that holds everything.
  • Redundancy does no harm: reframings preserve μβ\mu_\beta. Abelian classes all have size |G||V|−1|G|^{|V|-1}. You may pin a spanning tree to the identity before averaging invariants.
  • The stiffness dial runs from uniform labels (β=0\beta=0) to defect-free labelings (β→∞\beta\to\infty).
  • On a planar grid with the coin group, faces are independent coins with ⟨Uf⟩β=tanh⁡β\langle U_f\rangle_\beta=\tanh\beta.

What's next

A model is a distribution, but we can only ever ask it questions: "on average, what is this number?" Chapter 3 is about which questions make sense. Averages of non-invariant quantities, like a single edge label, turn out to be useless, and you'll see exactly why. Averages of loop products are where the structure shows up. On a planar grid, Fact 2.8 already tells you how the average loop product of a big rectangle must behave. Try to guess before you turn the page.
