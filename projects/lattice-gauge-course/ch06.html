Chapter 6 — Yang–Mills and the Millennium problem, in our terms

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute listed seven problems, each with a million-dollar prize. One of them is about physics: prove that quantum Yang–Mills theory exists and has a mass gap. It is usually stated in the language of quantum field theory: operator-valued distributions, Hilbert spaces, the Wightman axioms. That language takes years to learn.

This chapter shows that you already have almost everything you need to state the problem precisely. Yang–Mills theory on a grid is one of our models: a particular label group, a particular board, the standard face cost. The Millennium problem asks what happens to that model as the grid is refined. We will see where the physicists' formulas come from, run the experiment that first convinced physicists the picture is right, and end with a precise statement of what was proved in 2026 and what is still open.

What you'll learn

  • Why physics uses the groups U(1)U(1), SU(2)SU(2), SU(3)SU(3), and that lattice Yang–Mills is our model with G=SU(N)G=SU(N) on a four-dimensional grid.
  • Why the standard face cost is the right one, and how it becomes the physicists' "curvature" for smooth labelings, including the term that exists only because the labels don't commute.
  • Why four dimensions is special, and how a length scale appears out of nowhere (asymptotic freedom).
  • The Millennium problem, stated in the vocabulary of chapters 0–5, and its status as of November 2026.
  • What this elementary framework is missing for non-commutative physics: Lie algebras, representations, and how charges combine into invariants.

6.1 The label group of nature

The groups in physics' Standard Model are U(1)U(1), which we have met as the circle, and the matrix groups

SU(N)={g∈ℂN×N:g*g=I,det⁡g=1}. SU(N)=\lbrace g\in\mathbb C^{N\times N}: g^{*}g=I,\ \det g=1\rbrace .

SU(3)SU(3) is the label group of the strong force, which holds atomic nuclei together. We will work with the smallest case, SU(2)SU(2), because it already has everything that matters: it is compact, so it has a uniform distribution (chapter 2), and it is non-abelian, so reframing genuinely changes loop products, by conjugation (chapter 1).

SU(2)SU(2) has a friendly concrete form. Every element is wI−i(xσ1+yσ2+zσ3)wI-i(x\sigma_1+y\sigma_2+z\sigma_3) for a unit vector (w,x,y,z)∈ℝ4(w,x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^4, where σ1,σ2,σ3\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3 are three fixed 2×22\times2 matrices (the Pauli matrices). So SU(2)SU(2) is the 3-sphere {w2+x2+y2+z2=1}\lbrace w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2=1\rbrace, the matrix product, written in these coordinates, is the multiplication rule of the quaternions, and the trace is 2w2w. The library stores SU(2)SU(2) labels exactly this way.

Why these groups? The physics, at a high level

The interlude after chapter 1 gave the recipe. If some property of a particle can only be described relative to a convention, and every point of space may choose its convention independently, then comparing neighbors forces labels on edges. The label group is the group of convention changes for that property. So "which label group?" means "which hidden property, and how can its conventions be changed?" Physics answers this by experiment, three times:

Force Hidden property, and its conventions Label group
electromagnetism the phase of a charged particle's wavefunction: a unit complex number, with no preferred zero angle U(1)U(1), the circle
weak force a two-valued "which partner?" label: the electron and its neutrino (and the up and down quarks, the constituents of protons and neutrons) behave as two components of one vector in ℂ2\mathbb C^2 SU(2)SU(2)
strong force color: every quark carries a vector in ℂ3\mathbb C^3, with no preferred basis ("red, green, blue" is a convention) SU(3)SU(3)

A little history shows where non-commuting labels came from. In 1932 Heisenberg, noting that the proton and the neutron have almost the same mass, proposed treating them as two states of one particle. By 1937 experiments showed that they also feel the nuclear force almost identically, and this became a symmetry: the pair is one vector in ℂ2\mathbb C^2, and the force is unchanged when it is rotated by SU(2)SU(2) (physicists call this isospin). In 1954 Yang and Mills asked our interlude's question about it: what if every point could choose its own convention for which component is the proton? The answer was labels in a non-commuting group. That is where the name of this chapter comes from. Their own application did not survive: isospin is only an approximate symmetry, and their theory predicted massless force carriers that nobody had seen. But the structure later described both the weak and the strong force.

Why three colors? Nobody sees color directly, so the evidence is indirect, and it all says 3:

  • a particle called the Δ++\Delta^{++} is, in the simplest quark picture, three up quarks in the same state. Quarks are fermions, particles that quantum mechanics forbids from sharing a state with an identical twin (the Pauli exclusion principle). The puzzle disappears if the three quarks differ in a hidden label with at least three values;
  • when an electron and a positron (its antiparticle) collide, they sometimes produce hadrons (particles made of quarks) and sometimes a pair of muons (heavier cousins of the electron). The ratio of the two rates is three times what it would be without color, up to small corrections;
  • the neutral pion (a light particle made of quarks) decays into two photons, the particles of light, at a rate proportional to the square of the number of colors, and the measured rate matches 3.

And no free colored particle has ever been seen: only color-neutral combinations. In our language, only invariants are observed. That is chapter 3's statement that a model knows only averages of invariants, now as an experimental fact (physicists call it confinement of color).

Why "S" and "U"?

  • U (unitary, g*g=Ig^*g=I): a change of color convention must preserve the length of the color vector, because the length carries probability. Unitary matrices are exactly the length-preserving ones.
  • S (determinant 1): the remaining freedom in U(3)U(3), an overall phase eiαIe^{i\alpha}I, changes all three colors alike, so it is not a change of color convention at all. Including it would add a ninth force carrier that pulls on every quark equally, and no such force is seen; phases are left to the separate circle factor. Determinant 1 also has a structural consequence, which we will compute in section 6.6: three quarks can combine into an invariant. Protons and neutrons are built this way.
  • Not real rotations (SO(3)SO(3)): quarks and antiquarks (their antiparticles) carry genuinely different kinds of color charge, which needs complex vectors. With real rotations they would transform identically, and two quarks could already combine into an invariant (their dot product). Such two-quark particles are not seen.

To be honest about the limits of this story: these arguments explain what kind of group fits each force, and they simplify. The weak SU(2)SU(2) acts on only one "handedness" of each particle and is hidden at everyday energies, and the Standard Model's circle factor is not exactly electromagnetism's but mixes with SU(2)SU(2). Nor do the arguments explain why nature chose SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1)SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1) rather than something else. That remains an experimental input.

6.2 From faces to curvature

Physicists do not describe Yang–Mills theory with labels on edges. They use a gauge field AA: at every point of space, in every direction μ\mu, a small "infinitesimal rotation" Aμ(x)∈ℝ3A_\mu(x)\in\mathbb R^3 (for SU(2)SU(2)). Our labelings are the discrete version. Put the grid at spacing ε\varepsilon and set

U(x→x+εeμ)=exp⁡(εAμ(x))∈SU(2), U(x\to x+\varepsilon e_\mu)=\exp\big(\varepsilon A_\mu(x)\big)\in SU(2),

where exp⁡:ℝ3→SU(2)\exp:\mathbb R^3\to SU(2) turns a small rotation vector into a group element (v↦(cos⁡|v|,sin⁡|v|v/|v|)v\mapsto(\cos|v|,\ \sin|v|\,v/|v|) in quaternions; the library's SU2.exp). The labels are then all close to the identity, and change slowly from edge to edge.

What does our total cost become for such labels? Here is the answer first. It uses a field Fμν(x)∈ℝ3F_{\mu\nu}(x)\in\mathbb R^3 built from AA and its derivatives, the curvature, which Step 2 below constructs.

Fact 6.1 (faces become curvature). For slowly varying labels on a dd-dimensional grid of spacing ε\varepsilon, the total cost is S(U)=ε4−d∫∑μ<ν|Fμν(x)|2dx+O(ε6−d)S(U)=\varepsilon^{4-d}\displaystyle\int\sum_{\mu<\nu}|F_{\mu\nu}(x)|^2\,dx\ +\ O(\varepsilon^{6-d}).

We derive it in three steps.

Step 1: the cost of a label close to the identity

For SU(2)SU(2), exp⁡(v)\exp(v) has quaternion (cos⁡|v|,sin⁡|v|v/|v|)(\cos|v|,\ \sin|v|\,v/|v|) and trace 2cos⁡|v|2\cos|v|. So the standard cost of a label near the identity is

φ(exp⁡v)=2−2cos⁡|v|≈|v|2for small v. \varphi\big(\exp v\big)=2-2\cos|v|\approx|v|^2\qquad\text{for small }v .

The cost of a face whose product is exp⁡(v)\exp(v) is just the squared length of the rotation vector vv.

Step 2: the face product of slowly varying labels

Walk around the face at xx in the plane of directions μ,ν\mu,\nu. Its product is

Uf=exp⁡(εAμ(x))exp⁡(εAν(x+εeμ))exp⁡(−εAμ(x+εeν))exp⁡(−εAν(x)). U_f=\exp\big(\varepsilon A_\mu(x)\big)\,\exp\big(\varepsilon A_\nu(x+\varepsilon e_\mu)\big)\,\exp\big(-\varepsilon A_\mu(x+\varepsilon e_\nu)\big)\,\exp\big(-\varepsilon A_\nu(x)\big).

Two separate effects make UfU_f differ from the identity.

(a) The field changes from edge to edge. If the labels commuted, multiplying exponentials would just add their vectors. The sum is ε(Aμ(x)+Aν(x+εeμ)−Aμ(x+εeν)−Aν(x))\varepsilon\big(A_\mu(x)+A_\nu(x+\varepsilon e_\mu)-A_\mu(x+\varepsilon e_\nu)-A_\nu(x)\big), and a first-order Taylor expansion turns it into

ε2(∂μAν−∂νAμ). \varepsilon^2\big(\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu\big).

This is the "circulation around a small square" from vector calculus, the curl.

(b) The labels don't commute. Even if the field is constant, with Aμ=aA_\mu=a and Aν=bA_\nu=b everywhere, the face product is exp⁡(εa)exp⁡(εb)exp⁡(−εa)exp⁡(−εb)\exp(\varepsilon a)\exp(\varepsilon b)\exp(-\varepsilon a)\exp(-\varepsilon b). For numbers this would be exactly 1. For rotations it is not. Let's measure it.

def qlog(q):

As ε\varepsilon shrinks, the face product's rotation vector divided by ε2\varepsilon^2 approaches 2a×b2\,a\times b, with an error that halves along with ε\varepsilon:

Fact 6.2 (small loops measure non-commutativity). For small rotation vectors, exp⁡(εa)exp⁡(εb)exp⁡(−εa)exp⁡(−εb)=exp⁡(ε2[a,b]+O(ε3))\exp(\varepsilon a)\exp(\varepsilon b)\exp(-\varepsilon a)\exp(-\varepsilon b)=\exp\big(\varepsilon^2[a,b]+O(\varepsilon^3)\big), where for SU(2)SU(2) the bracket is [a,b]=2a×b[a,b]=2\,a\times b.

The space of small rotation vectors, ℝ3\mathbb R^3 here, together with this bracket is what mathematicians call the Lie algebra of the group. The bracket is the infinitesimal shadow of non-commutativity. It vanishes for the circle, where every two labels commute. (The general tool behind Fact 6.2 is the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula.)

To order ε2\varepsilon^2 the two effects simply add: the bracket in (b) is already of order ε2\varepsilon^2, so evaluating the field at the face's corner xx instead of at each edge changes it only at order ε3\varepsilon^3. So the face product is exp⁡(ε2Fμν(x))\exp\big(\varepsilon^2F_{\mu\nu}(x)\big) up to smaller terms, where

Fμν=∂μAν−∂νAμ+2Aμ×Aν∈ℝ3. F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu+2\,A_\mu\times A_\nu\ \in\mathbb R^3 .

The first two terms are effect (a); the cross product is effect (b), and it exists only because the labels don't commute. Physicists call FF the curvature or field strength.

Step 3: add up the faces

By Step 1, each face costs about |ε2F|2=ε4|Fμν(x)|2|\varepsilon^2F|^2=\varepsilon^4|F_{\mu\nu}(x)|^2. A dd-dimensional region of unit volume contains about ε−d\varepsilon^{-d} grid vertices, and one face per vertex in each plane μ<ν\mu<\nu. So summing over faces is ε−d\varepsilon^{-d} times integrating:

S(U)≈∑x∑μ<νε4|Fμν(x)|2≈ε4−d∫∑μ<ν|Fμν(x)|2dx. S(U)\approx\sum_{x}\sum_{\mu<\nu}\varepsilon^4|F_{\mu\nu}(x)|^2\approx\varepsilon^{4-d}\int\sum_{\mu<\nu}|F_{\mu\nu}(x)|^2\,dx .

This is Fact 6.1. The integral is the Yang–Mills energy (physicists: the Yang–Mills action). Let's verify Fact 6.1 in two dimensions, where S≈ε2∫|F12|2S\approx\varepsilon^{2}\int|F_{12}|^2, using a smooth field of our choosing.

def A_field(x, y):
    """A smooth SU(2) gauge field on the unit square: two R^3-valued functions."""
    a1 = np.stack([np.sin(2 * np.pi * y), 0.5 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * x), 0 * x], -1)
    a2 = np.stack([0 * x, np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * y), 0.7 + 0 * x], -1)
    return a1, a2

def curvature(x, y, with_cross=True, h=1e-5):
    a1, a2 = A_field(x, y)
    d1a2 = (A_field(x + h, y)[1] - A_field(x - h, y)[1]) / (2 * h)
    d2a1 = (A_field(x, y + h)[0] - A_field(x, y - h)[0]) / (2 * h)
    return d1a2 - d2a1 + (2 * np.cross(a1, a2) if with_cross else 0)
def smooth_labeling(n):
    """The labeling U(x -> x + eps e_mu) = exp(eps A_mu(x)) on an n x n torus, eps = 1/n."""

Why this face cost?

The standard face cost φ(x)=N−Re⁡tr⁡x\varphi(x)=N-\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}x can look arbitrary. It isn't. Here are the requirements it meets, each one forced by something earlier in the course:

  1. Reframing-invariant. A model must not depend on conventions (chapter 2). The cost has to be an invariant, and the most local building blocks are face products. (Locality is what makes sampling cheap in chapter 4 and gives the transfer matrix of chapter 5; costs that couple distant faces lose both.)
  2. A class function. Reframing changes a face product by conjugation, Uf↦hUfh−1U_f\mapsto h\,U_f\,h^{-1} (chapter 1). So φ\varphi must satisfy φ(hxh−1)=φ(x)\varphi(hxh^{-1})=\varphi(x). For SU(2)SU(2) this means φ\varphi depends only on the rotation angle of the face product, not on its axis. The trace is the simplest class function there is.
  3. Smallest at the identity, and only there. Calm faces should be preferred, so consistent labelings are the most likely.
  4. The right behavior near the identity. Step 1 of section 6.2 used φ(exp⁡v)≈|v|2\varphi(\exp v)\approx|v|^2. That is what makes the total cost become the Yang–Mills energy.
  5. Flip-positive. Its weight e−βφe^{-\beta\varphi} expands in characters with nonnegative coefficients (chapter 5, section 5.6), so the derived system exists.

Requirement 4 hides the deepest point: only the bottom of the bowl matters. For smooth labelings, every face product is near the identity, and there any cost meeting requirements 2–4 looks like |v|2|v|^2. Far from the identity, different costs disagree, but the continuum limit is believed not to care.   • the heat-kernel cost makes the 2D model exactly unchanged when a face is subdivided, so in 2D every grid already gives the continuum answer;
  • improved costs add small 1×21\times2 rectangles with tuned weights, which cancels the leading ε2\varepsilon^2 error of Fact 6.1, so coarser grids already look continuous;
  • the standard cost, introduced by Kenneth Wilson in 1974, is the simplest, and it is what we have used.

Why is ∫|F|2\int|F|^2 itself the right continuum energy? Run the same requirements on continuum expressions. The energy must be built from FF (and its derivatives), because AA itself carries the conventions. It must be unchanged by reframings, local, and unchanged by rotations of space. Among such expressions, ∫|F|2\int|F|^2 is the simplest one. In 4D it is also the only one whose stiffness is a pure number (section 6.3); every more complicated expression, such as ∫|F|4\int|F|^4, gets suppressed by extra powers of ε\varepsilon as the grid is refined. (There is one exception: a "topological" term, called the θ\theta-term, whose value does not change under small changes of the field. It is outside this course.)

Recap. 6.3 Why four dimensions is special

Look at the power of ε\varepsilon in Fact 6.1. The model's weight is

e−βS≈exp⁡(−βε4−d∫|F|2). e^{-\beta S}\approx\exp\Big(-\beta\,\varepsilon^{4-d}\int|F|^2\Big).

To describe a fixed continuous picture as the grid is refined, the combination βε4−d\beta\,\varepsilon^{4-d} should stay fixed. In d=2d=2 or 33 that means β\beta grows as ε→0\varepsilon\to0 in a definite way. But in d=4d=4 the spacing cancels: β\beta is a pure number, and nothing in the formula says how big anything is. Classically, four-dimensional Yang–Mills has no built-in length scale. A theory with no length scale cannot have a mass gap: there is no length to set the size of a lightest particle.

Yet the model on the grid does have a length: its correlation length ξ(β)\xi(\beta), measured in grid steps (chapter 5's inverse gap). The resolution is that the naive scaling is wrong. The randomness of the labels at short distances changes how β\beta must be tuned. The physicists' computation, done by hand in 1973 by Gross, Wilczek and Politzer, says that to keep the physics fixed while refining, the stiffness must grow, slowly:

ε≈1Λe−6π2β/11(SU(2),our β,leading order). \varepsilon\approx\frac{1}{\Lambda}\,e^{-6\pi^2\beta/11}\qquad(SU(2),\ \text{our }\beta,\ \text{leading order}).

Here 1/Λ1/\Lambda is a length scale that the theory creates: it is not in the definition. This is asymptotic freedom: as the grid gets finer the stiffness goes to infinity, so on short scales the labels are nearly consistent. The number 1111 comes from counting how the non-commuting labels interact, which is the cross term in FF (Fact 6.2) at work. Equivalently, the area-law rate per face (the rate cc in chapter 3's decay ⟨W⟩≈e−c⋅area\langle W\rangle\approx e^{-c\cdot\text{area}}; physicists call it the "string tension" in grid units) should fall like e−12π2β/11e^{-12\pi^2\beta/11}.

What "running" means, concretely

The phrase "the stiffness must grow" is easy to say and hard to picture. Here it is step by step.

  1. Pick something physical to hold fixed. For example, the correlation length: how far apart two faces must be before their behaviors become (nearly) independent. Measure it in physical units, say LphysL_{\text{phys}}.
  2. On the grid, the model measures it in grid steps. The model at stiffness β\beta has some correlation length ξ(β)\xi(\beta) in grid steps, the inverse of chapter 5's gap. In physical units that is εξ(β)\varepsilon\,\xi(\beta).
  3. Refine the grid without changing the physics. Keeping εξ(β)=Lphys\varepsilon\,\xi(\beta)=L_{\text{phys}} while ε→0\varepsilon\to0 forces ξ(β)=Lphys/ε→∞\xi(\beta)=L_{\text{phys}}/\varepsilon\to\infty. The model must be tuned to stiffer and stiffer values, where its correlations reach across more and more grid steps.
  4. How fast? Asymptotic freedom says ξ(β)\xi(\beta) grows exponentially with β\beta, like e6π2β/11e^{6\pi^2\beta/11}. So refining by a factor 2 costs only an additive Δβ≈0.13\Delta\beta\approx0.13, and β(ε)\beta(\varepsilon) grows like 116π2ln⁡(1/ε)\frac{11}{6\pi^2}\ln(1/\varepsilon): logarithmically slowly.

Why must β\beta grow, rather than shrink? Here is a heuristic picture, not a proof; the direction of the effect is the output of the 1973 calculation, not something you can see without it. Imagine viewing a fine grid from twice as far away, by combining the labels of each 2×22\times2 block of faces into "coarse" labels. For non-commuting groups the random wiggles of the fine labels interact with each other, through the cross term of Fact 6.2, and their net effect is to make the coarse labels rougher than the fine ones: the coarse grid behaves like a model with a smaller stiffness. So to make the coarse picture match a given target, the fine grid must start stiffer. Physicists call this anti-screening, and counting the contributions is the calculation that produced the 11.

The direction depends on the group. For the circle alone, the wiggles at different scales do not interact (at large β\beta in 4D the model is nearly Gaussian) and the stiffness does not run at all. In real electromagnetism, pairs of charged particles make it run the other way: the effective stiffness grows at long distances, and electric charge looks weaker from far away (physicists call this screening).

The experimental signature: at very short distances, which means at very high energies, quarks inside a proton behave as if they were almost free. This was seen in electron–proton collisions at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC) from 1968 onward ("deep inelastic scattering", Nobel Prize 1990 for Friedman, Kendall and Taylor), before the theory explained it. Gross, Wilczek and Politzer received the 2004 Nobel Prize for the explanation.

Recap. In 4D the total cost of a smooth labeling does not depend on the spacing, so classically there is no length scale. Asymptotic freedom says the stiffness must grow logarithmically as the grid is refined, and a length scale 1/Λ1/\Lambda appears from the randomness itself.

6.5 The Millennium problem, in our terms

Here is the plan of the construction, using only words from this course.

The refinement. Fix a compact, simple, non-abelian label group GG (for example SU(2)SU(2) or SU(3)SU(3)). For a spacing ε>0\varepsilon>0, take the four-dimensional torus grid of spacing ε\varepsilon and side LL (in physical units), with the standard face cost and some stiffness β(ε)\beta(\varepsilon). This is a model με\mu_\varepsilon in the sense of chapter 2: an honest probability distribution on GEG^E, a finite product of copies of GG (of 3-spheres, for SU(2)SU(2)). Then send ε→0\varepsilon\to0 and L→∞L\to\infty.

Existence. Show that β(ε)\beta(\varepsilon) can be chosen so that:

  1. averages of invariants (suitably smoothed loop observables) converge as ε→0\varepsilon\to0, L→∞L\to\infty;
  2. the limit is nontrivial: not the model where all labels are consistent, and not a "free" (Gaussian) model;
  3. the limit keeps flip-positivity (chapter 5, glossary 5.7), which survives limits;
  4. the limit gains the full symmetry of 4D space: all rotations and translations, not only the quarter-turns and whole-step shifts of the grid.

Given 1–4, the construction of chapter 5 (state space from functions of a layer, H=−log⁡TH=-\log T) goes through in the limit. Physicists call this Osterwalder–Schrader reconstruction, and the result is what they mean by "quantum Yang–Mills theory on ℝ4\mathbb R^4".

Mass gap. In the limit, the derived system has levels 0=E0≤E1≤…0=E_0\le E_1\le\dots, and the problem asks to show

Δ=E1>0. \Delta=E_1>0 .

In grid language: the gap measured in grid units, E1(β)E_1(\beta), times the inverse spacing, stays bounded away from zero, E1(β(ε))/ε→Δ>0E_1(\beta(\varepsilon))/\varepsilon\to\Delta>0. Equivalently, the correlation length measured in physical units, εξ(β(ε))\varepsilon\,\xi(\beta(\varepsilon)), stays bounded (it tends to 1/Δ1/\Delta). Physically: the lightest particle of the theory (a "glueball", a lump of pure field) has a positive mass, even though the classical theory has no length scale at all (section 6.3).

Chatterjee's survey Yang–Mills for probabilists states the probabilistic core as three problems (its Problems 4.1, 5.1 and 5.2), which now read naturally. Each is about any infinite-volume limit of the 4D model, for non-abelian GG:

6.6 What our elementary framework is missing

Everything in chapters 0–5 works for any compact group, commuting or not: labelings, reframings, classes, models, sampling, layers. So in one sense the framework already handles non-commutative labels. But physics with non-commuting groups uses several tools we never built. Some appeared in this chapter in disguise. Here they are, in roughly the order you would need them.

1. The infinitesimal picture: Lie algebras

Section 6.2 used "small rotation vectors" v∈ℝ3v\in\mathbb R^3, the map exp\exp, and the bracket [a,b]=2a×b[a,b]=2\,a\times b, all defined by hand for SU(2)SU(2). The general theory is the Lie algebra of a group: the vectors that exp\exp turns into group elements near the identity, with the bracket from Fact 6.2. It is what lets you write a continuum field Aμ(x)A_\mu(x), a continuum reframing h(x)=exp⁡(λ(x))h(x)=\exp(\lambda(x)), and the curvature FF for any group. For SU(N)SU(N) the Lie algebra has dimension N2−1N^2-1: 3 for SU(2)SU(2), and 8 for SU(3)SU(3). Those 8 directions are the 8 kinds of gluon, the particles that carry the strong force as photons carry electromagnetism.

2. Charged fields that are vectors: representations

The interlude's charged field had one complex number per vertex, which is enough for the circle. A quark carries a color vector, ψ(v)∈ℂN\psi(v)\in\mathbb C^N, and a reframing acts on it by a matrix. With ψ\psi written as a row vector, the rule that keeps the interlude's formulas unchanged is

ψ(v)↦ψ(v)h(v)−1. \psi(v)\longmapsto\psi(v)\,h(v)^{-1}.

The covariant difference has the same formula as before, ψ(s)U(s→t)−ψ(t)\psi(s)\,U(s\to t)-\psi(t), with UU now a matrix. A way for a group to act by matrices on vectors is called a representation. A group has many representations, and different particles live in different ones:

  • quarks are in the defining representation ℂN\mathbb C^N;
  • gluons themselves are in the adjoint representation: the Lie algebra, of dimension N2−1N^2-1, on which hh acts by conjugation, v↦hvh−1v\mapsto hvh^{-1}.

The loop observables of chapter 3 can then be taken in any representation: χ\chi becomes the trace of the loop product as it acts in that representation. Different representations give different area-law rates. In 2D, for SU(2)SU(2), the adjoint rate approaches 8/38/3 times the defining one as β→∞\beta\to\infty (and equals it exactly for the heat-kernel cost).

3. Building invariants from several charges

Experiments only see invariants. With vector-valued charges, which combinations are invariants? Two examples show the pattern.

A quark and an antiquark joined by a string. Take a quark at ss, with color vector ψ(s)\psi(s), and a walk γ\gamma from ss to tt. An antiquark at tt is described by a column vector η(t)*\eta(t)^{*}, the conjugate transpose of a row vector η(t)\eta(t) that reframes like a quark; since hh is unitary, η(t)*↦h(t)η(t)*\eta(t)^{*}\mapsto h(t)\,\eta(t)^{*}. The number

ψ(s)U(γ)η(t)*∈ℂ \psi(s)\,U(\gamma)\,\eta(t)^{*}\in\mathbb C

is an invariant. The reframings at ss and tt cancel against the endpoints of the walk product, the same telescoping as in chapter 1. A single quark has no invariant at all, but a quark–antiquark pair joined by a path of labels does. Physicists call such a pair a meson. The string of labels between them is the precise sense in which charges in a gauge theory are never fully local, and chapter 3's area law is the cost of stretching that string.

Two (or three) quarks at one point. For SU(2)SU(2), two color vectors ψ,η∈ℂ2\psi,\eta\in\mathbb C^2 (rows) at the same vertex give the invariant

det⁡(ψη), \det\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\ \eta\end{pmatrix},

because a reframing multiplies the 2×22\times2 matrix on the right by h−1h^{-1}, which multiplies its determinant by det⁡h−1=1\det h^{-1}=1. Here is where the "S" of SUSU earns its place: for unitary matrices with det⁡h≠1\det h\neq1, this would not be an invariant. For SU(3)SU(3) the same construction with three color vectors gives the invariant det⁡(ψ1;ψ2;ψ3)\det(\psi_1;\psi_2;\psi_3): three quarks combine into a color-neutral particle, a baryon. Protons and neutrons are baryons.

The string invariant survives the reframing exactly. The determinant survives an SU(2)SU(2) reframing but not one with det⁡h≠1\det h\neq1.

Classifying all the ways to build invariants from several representations is the subject of representation theory: tensor products, characters, and their orthogonality. It is the main mathematical tool we skipped. It also powers the exact solution of the 2D model for non-commuting groups (a random walk on GG, diagonalized by characters, just as section 5.7 did for the circle), the expansion behind flip-positivity (section 5.6), and predictions such as the ratio 8/38/3 between the area-law rates of adjoint and defining loops in 2D (a ratio of two numbers attached to each representation, 22 and 3/43/4).

4. Two things that need more than new tools

  • Quarks are fermions. Their path-integral variables anticommute (Grassmann variables), so their "weights" are not probabilities. They can be integrated out into a determinant, but that determinant is not always positive (it is in the cases lattice simulations of the strong force mostly use, but not, for example, for matter at high density), which breaks the stance "a model is a probability distribution". Putting them on a grid also hits a no-go theorem (Nielsen–Ninomiya): under natural assumptions, left-right asymmetric matter like the weak force's cannot be put on a grid directly.
  • Topology. Smooth fields with labels in SU(2)SU(2), the 3-sphere, can wind around the group in ways no small smooth change can undo (instantons). They carry the θ\theta-term mentioned in section 6.2 and are invisible to the face-by-face reasoning of this course. (On a grid the winding is only approximately defined, since any labeling can be deformed continuously into any other.)

Recap. To go beyond pure Yang–Mills on a grid you need: Lie algebras (the infinitesimal side: small rotation vectors and the bracket), representations (charged fields that are vectors; quarks in ℂN\mathbb C^N, gluons in the Lie algebra), and representation theory (which combinations of charges are invariant: mesons as quark–string–antiquark, baryons as determinants, which is why det⁡=1\det=1 matters). Fermions and topology need genuinely new ideas.

Answer

A reframing multiplies ψ(v)\psi(v) by h(v)−1h(v)^{-1}, which changes it. In the string expression, the h(s)−1h(s)^{-1} from the quark cancels the h(s)h(s) at the start of the walk product, and the h(t)−1h(t)^{-1} at the end of the walk product cancels the h(t)h(t) from the antiquark.

Answer

Not by the determinant: it would pick up the factor det⁡h−1\det h^{-1}, a phase that need not be 1.

Course recap

  • Chapter 0: a currency market is a board with labels; round trips are the only meaningful numbers; changing units is a reframing.
  • Chapter 1: for any group, labelings modulo reframings are classes; invariants are functions of classes; loop products change by conjugation; the tree normal form removes the redundancy.
  • Chapter 2: a model is a probability distribution ∝e−βS\propto e^{-\beta S} over labelings, built on the uniform distribution of a compact group; redundancy costs nothing.
  • Chapter 3: a model knows only averages of invariants; single labels average to zero; loop observables reveal the area law.
  • Chapter 4: Markov chains sample models using only local information, and found a jump at a number we can derive, 12ln⁡(1+2)\tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2).
  • Chapter 5: cutting a board into layers turns a model into a transfer matrix; its levels, the "quantum system", are derived from the distribution alone.
  • Chapter 6: physics' label groups are the convention-change groups of hidden properties (phase, weak partner, color). Yang–Mills is our model with SU(N)SU(N); the standard cost is the simplest valid one and becomes curvature for smooth labels, with a term that exists only because labels don't commute. 4D has no built-in scale until asymptotic freedom creates one; the Millennium problem is a statement about refining that model. Going further needs Lie algebras and representation theory.
