Chapter 7 — A gap you can prove

Two things in this course have been forgetting. In chapter 4, the heat-bath sampler started from an arbitrary labeling and, after enough sweeps, forgot where it started. In chapter 5, two layers of a board forgot each other at a rate we called the gap. We measured both, but proved neither.

This chapter proves that they are the same phenomenon, and turns it into a theorem. If every edge listens only weakly to its neighbors, then two samplers fed the same random numbers are forced to agree. From that one fact follow decaying correlations and then a positive gap, with an explicit number. The result holds for every compact label group, every dimension and every board width. It is the small-stiffness end of the mass-gap story of chapter 6 (physicists say "strong coupling"), proved from scratch. Its method is a standard tool of computer science, the coupling argument used to show that Markov chain samplers run fast.

The last section is the honest part. It explains exactly why this theorem, and every theorem of its kind, stops short of the Millennium problem. It also explains what the 2026 existence proof does and does not supply toward closing the distance.

What you'll learn

  • How much one edge "listens" to another (its influence), and the one-number test α<1\alpha<1.
  • How running two samplers on the same random numbers proves that they forget their starting points. This is the coupling method of computer science.
  • How forgetting gives decaying correlations and then a gap E1≥ln⁡(1/α)E_1\ge\ln(1/\alpha), and why no argument of this kind reaches the continuum.

Prerequisites: chapters 2 (models), 4 (heat bath, face remainders) and 5 (transfer matrix, levels). The last section uses chapter 6.

7.1 How much does one edge listen to another?

Recall the heat bath (chapter 4). To update one edge ee, you hold every other label fixed and draw U(e)U(e) from its conditional distribution. That distribution depends only on the face remainders of the faces through ee (glossary 4.8):

μe(dx∣U)∝exp⁡(−β∑f∋eφ(xrf(e)))σ(dx). \mu_e(dx\mid U)\ \propto\ \exp\Big(-\beta\sum_{f\ni e}\varphi\big(x\,r_f(e)\big)\Big)\,\sigma(dx).

Change the label of some other edge e′e', and the conditional distribution at ee may move. If ee and e′e' share no face, it does not move at all. If they share a face, it moves a little. To say "a little" precisely, we need a distance between distributions.

Definition (distance between distributions). For two distributions p,qp,q on the same set, d(p,q)∈[0,1]d(p,q)\in[0,1] is the largest difference in probability they assign to any event:

d(p,q)=maxA|p(A)−q(A)|=12∑x|p(x)−q(x)|. d(p,q)=\max_{A}\,\big|p(A)-q(A)\big|=\tfrac12\sum_x|p(x)-q(x)| .

For distributions with densities, the sum becomes an integral. (Probabilists call this the total variation distance.)

Definition (influence). For edges e≠e′e\neq e', the influence of e′e' on ee is C(e,e′)∈[0,1]C(e,e')\in[0,1]:

C(e,e′)=max⁡{d(μe(⋅∣U),μe(⋅∣U′)):U,U′ differ only at e′}. C(e,e')=\max\Big\lbrace d\big(\mu_e(\cdot\mid U),\,\mu_e(\cdot\mid U')\big)\ :\ U,U'\text{ differ only at }e'\Big\rbrace .

Set C(e,e)=0C(e,e)=0. The influence number of the model is the most any edge listens in total:

α=maxe∑e′C(e,e′). \alpha=\max_e\sum_{e'}C(e,e').

Finally, the influence distance between two sets of edges is the fewest steps between them, where one step moves between two edges that share a face. (Probabilists call CC Dobrushin's interdependence matrix, and α<1\alpha<1 Dobrushin's condition.)

The influence of one face on an edge is controlled by a small lemma about tilting a distribution. Here tanh\operatorname{tanh} is the hyperbolic tangent; for small cc, tanh⁡(c/4)≈c/4\tanh(c/4)\approx c/4.

Fact 7.1 (tilting by a bounded factor). Let pp be a distribution and gg a positive function whose largest value is at most ece^{c} times its smallest. Let pg∝gpp_g\propto g\,p be pp reweighted by gg. Then d(p,pg)≤tanh⁡(c/4)d(p,p_g)\le\tanh(c/4), with equality for a well-chosen two-point pp.

Proof. Rescale gg so that it takes values in [b,1][b,1] with b=e−cb=e^{-c}, and let m=⟨g⟩p∈[b,1]m=\langle g\rangle_p\in[b,1]. Then

d(p,pg)=⟨(1−g/m)+⟩p. d(p,p_g)=\big\langle\big(1-g/m\big)_+\big\rangle_p .

Fix mm. The function g↦(1−g/m)+g\mapsto(1-g/m)_+ is convex, so it lies below its chord between bb and 11. At g=1g=1 it is 00, because m≤1m\le1. Averaging the chord gives d≤s(1−b/m)d\le s\,(1-b/m), where ss is the weight the chord puts on bb; the mean constraint forces s=(1−m)/(1−b)s=(1-m)/(1-b). Substituting,

d≤(1−m)(m−b)(1−b)m=1+b−(m+b/m)1−b≤1+b−2b1−b=1−b1+b=tanh⁡c4, d\le\frac{(1-m)(m-b)}{(1-b)\,m}=\frac{1+b-(m+b/m)}{1-b}\le\frac{1+b-2\sqrt b}{1-b}=\frac{1-\sqrt b}{1+\sqrt b}=\tanh\frac c4,

using m+b/m≥2bm+b/m\ge2\sqrt b. Equality holds when gg takes only the values bb and 11 and m=bm=\sqrt b. ▫\square

Now apply it to the grid. Let Φ=max⁡φ−min⁡φ\Phi=\max\varphi-\min\varphi be the spread of the face cost. For the coin model, Φ=2\Phi=2. For SU(2)SU(2) with φ=2−tr\varphi=2-\operatorname{tr}, Φ=4\Phi=4.

Fact 7.2 (influence on a grid). On a dd-dimensional torus grid with every side at least 3, take any compact GG and any face cost of spread Φ\Phi. Then C(e,e′)≤tanh⁡(βΦ/2)C(e,e')\le\tanh(\beta\Phi/2) when ee and e′e' share a face, and C(e,e′)=0C(e,e')=0 otherwise. Every edge shares a face with exactly 6(d−1)6(d-1) other edges. So the influence number satisfies α≤6(d−1)tanh⁡(βΦ/2)\alpha\le6(d-1)\tanh(\beta\Phi/2).

Proof. With side at least 3, two edges share at most one face ff. Changing U(e′)U(e') changes only rf(e)r_f(e), so it reweights μe\mu_e by g(x)=exp⁡(−β[φ(xrf′)−φ(xrf)])g(x)=\exp\big(-\beta[\varphi(xr_f')-\varphi(xr_f)]\big). The exponent lies in [−βΦ,βΦ][-\beta\Phi,\beta\Phi], so max⁡g/min⁡g≤e2βΦ\max g/\min g\le e^{2\beta\Phi}. Fact 7.1 with c=2βΦc=2\beta\Phi gives the bound. For the count: ee lies in 2(d−1)2(d-1) faces, each with 3 other edges, all distinct. ▫\square

Let's check Fact 7.2 against the actual conditional distributions. For the coin model we can try every configuration of the remainders. For SU(2)SU(2) we draw random remainders, including the worst kind of change (one remainder flipped to its negative), and compute the distance by sampling the 3-sphere.

def coin_influence(beta, d):
    """Exact worst influence for the coin model: P(+1) = logistic(2 beta * sum of remainders)."""
    worst = 0.0
    for rs in itertools.product([1, -1], repeat=2 * (d - 1)):
        A, A2 = sum(rs), sum(rs) - 2 * rs[0]           # change the first face's remainder
        p, p2 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * beta * A)), 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * beta * A2))
        worst = max(worst, abs(p - p2))
    return worst

def su2_influence(beta, d, trials=60, n=100_000):
    """Sampled worst influence for SU(2): density on S^3 proportional to exp(2 beta <x, sum of remainders>)."""
    G, worst = SU2(), 0.0
    x = G.uniform(rng, (n,))
    for _ in range(trials):
        r = G.uniform(rng, (2 * (d - 1),)); r2 = r.copy(); r2[0] = -r[0]
        w1, w2 = np.exp(2 * beta * x @ r.sum(0)), np.exp(2 * beta * x @ r2.sum(0))
        worst = max(worst, 0.5 * np.mean(np.abs(w1 / w1.mean() - w2 / w2.mean())))
    return worst

7.2 Two samplers, one set of dice

Here is the idea that powers the rest of the chapter. Run two heat-bath samplers side by side, started from different labelings, and feed them the same random numbers. Whenever their conditional distributions at an edge are close, the shared randomness makes them pick the same label. Disagreements can then only survive by being passed from neighbor to neighbor. If every edge listens to its neighbors with total strength α<1\alpha<1, disagreements die faster than they spread.

Definition (coupling). A coupling of two distributions p,qp,q is a pair of random variables (X,Y)(X,Y), drawn jointly, with X∼pX\sim p and Y∼qY\sim q. For any coupling, P(X≠Y)≥d(p,q)P(X\neq Y)\ge d(p,q). There is always a coupling with P(X≠Y)=d(p,q)P(X\neq Y)=d(p,q), called a best coupling:

  • with probability 1−d1-d, draw one value from the overlap min⁡(p,q)\min(p,q) (normalized) and set X=YX=Y to it;
  • otherwise draw XX and YY from the two leftover parts.

For the coin model the heat bath draws +1+1 when a uniform random number uu falls below P(+1)P(+1). Two samplers using the same uu disagree exactly when uu falls between their two values of P(+1)P(+1), which happens with probability |P(+1)−P′(+1)|=d|P(+1)-P'(+1)|=d. So "same random numbers" is the best coupling.

Fact 7.3 (coupled samplers forget). Run two heat-bath samplers of the same model, updating edges in the same order, and use a best coupling at every update. Let ρt(e)\rho_t(e) be the probability that the two labels at ee differ after tt sweeps. Then max⁡eρt(e)≤αtmax⁡eρ0(e)\max_e\rho_t(e)\le\alpha^t\,\max_e\rho_0(e).

Proof. Look at one update of edge ee, with current labelings XX and YY. Walk from XX to YY by changing their differing labels one at a time. Each change of a label at e′e' moves the conditional distribution at ee by at most C(e,e′)C(e,e'), so

d(μe(⋅∣X),μe(⋅∣Y))≤∑e′C(e,e′)𝟏[X(e′)≠Y(e′)]. d\big(\mu_e(\cdot\mid X),\mu_e(\cdot\mid Y)\big)\ \le\ \sum_{e'}C(e,e')\,\mathbf 1[X(e')\neq Y(e')].

The best coupling makes the new labels differ with exactly that probability. Averaging over X,YX,Y, the new ρ(e)\rho(e) is at most ∑e′C(e,e′)ρ(e′)≤αmax⁡ρ\sum_{e'}C(e,e')\rho(e')\le\alpha\max\rho. So the maximum never grows during a sweep. After a full sweep every edge has been updated once, so every ρ(e)\rho(e) is at most α\alpha times the maximum at the start of the sweep. ▫\square

Fact 7.3 is the first appearance of the computer-science bridge. If one sampler starts from the model itself, it stays distributed as the model, so the other sampler's labels agree with a true sample except with probability at most αt\alpha^t. With |E||E| edges, after t≈log⁡|E|/log⁡(1/α)t\approx\log|E|/\log(1/\alpha) sweeps every label agrees. (Computer scientists call this the path-coupling proof of rapid mixing. Its best-known use shows that the heat bath for graph colorings mixes fast when there are more than 2Δ2\Delta colors, Δ\Delta the maximum degree. The structure is the same: bounded degree, weak dependence.)

Let's watch it happen on the 4D coin model. One sampler starts with every label +1+1, the other with random labels; both use the same random numbers.

def coupled_disagreement(beta, L=6, sweeps=30, seeds=4):
    """Fraction of edges where two coupled heat-bath samplers disagree, after each sweep."""
    runs = []
    for seed in range(seeds):
        X = GridModel((L,) * 4, Z2(), beta).cold_start()
        Y = GridModel((L,) * 4, Z2(), beta).hot_start(np.random.default_rng(100 + seed))
        rX, rY = np.random.default_rng(seed), np.random.default_rng(seed)   # same dice
        frac = [np.mean(X.U != Y.U)]
        for _ in range(sweeps):
            X.sweep(rX); Y.sweep(rY)
            frac.append(np.mean(X.U != Y.U))
        runs.append(frac)
    return np.mean(runs, axis=0)

Look at the two regimes. For α<1\alpha<1 (teal, ink), disagreements vanish entirely within a few sweeps, below the dashed guarantee. (The plot floors exact zero at 10−510^{-5}.) For β=0.2\beta=0.2 and 0.60.6, Fact 7.2 gives no guarantee, and the disagreements never die. They fail in two different ways:

  • At β=0.2\beta=0.2, below the jump, the two samplers disagree on about 40% of their face products too. Each sampler still forgets its start (chapter 4 checked this by other means). But this particular coupling no longer forces them together. A failed certificate is not proof of slow forgetting.

Recap. A coupling runs two random processes on shared randomness. With the best coupling at every update, the chance that two heat-bath samplers disagree at any edge shrinks by a factor α\alpha per sweep. When α<1\alpha<1 the sampler forgets its start geometrically fast.

7.3 From forgetting to decorrelation

Forgetting in time (sweeps of a sampler) now gives forgetting in space (distance on the board). The trick: a correlation between AA and BB measures how much conditioning on BB moves the average of AA. Conditioning on BB is the same as reweighting the model by BB. A reweighted model is a new model whose conditional distributions agree with the old one at every edge that BB does not read. So we can couple a sampler of the old model with a sampler of the reweighted one. Their disagreements are created only on BB's edges, and they fade with influence distance.

For a function AA of a labeling, write osc⁡(A)=max⁡A−min⁡A\operatorname{osc}(A)=\max A-\min A. Say AA reads a set of edges SAS_A if it depends only on their labels.

Fact 7.4 (decorrelation). Suppose α<1\alpha<1. Let AA read SAS_A, let BB read SBS_B, and let DD be the influence distance between SAS_A and SBS_B. Then the correlation of AA and BB is at most osc⁡(A)osc⁡(B)|SA|αD/(1−α)\operatorname{osc}(A)\,\operatorname{osc}(B)\,|S_A|\,\alpha^{D}/(1-\alpha) in absolute value.

In one line:

|⟨AB⟩−⟨A⟩⟨B⟩|≤osc⁡(A)osc⁡(B)|SA|αD1−α. \big|\langle AB\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle\big|\ \le\ \operatorname{osc}(A)\,\operatorname{osc}(B)\,|S_A|\,\frac{\alpha^{D}}{1-\alpha}.

Proof.

Step 1 (reweight). Replace BB by B′=B−min⁡B≥0B'=B-\min B\ge0; this does not change the correlation. If ⟨B′⟩=0\langle B'\rangle=0 the correlation is 00. Otherwise let ν\nu be the model reweighted by B′B', that is, ν(dU)=B′(U)μ(dU)/⟨B′⟩\nu(dU)=B'(U)\mu(dU)/\langle B'\rangle. Then ⟨AB⟩−⟨A⟩⟨B⟩=⟨B′⟩(⟨A⟩ν−⟨A⟩)\langle AB\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle=\langle B'\rangle\big(\langle A\rangle_\nu-\langle A\rangle\big), and ⟨B′⟩≤osc⁡(B)\langle B'\rangle\le\operatorname{osc}(B).

Step 2 (same conditionals off SBS_B). At an edge e∉SBe\notin S_B, the factor B′B' does not depend on U(e)U(e). It cancels from the conditional distribution, so ν\nu and μ\mu have the same heat bath at ee.

Step 3 (couple). Run a heat-bath sampler XX of μ\mu and one YY of ν\nu, started from samples of μ\mu and ν\nu, so that at every time X∼μX\sim\mu and Y∼νY\sim\nu. Use best couplings. An update at ee produces a disagreement with probability at most ∑e′C(e,e′)ρ(e′)\sum_{e'}C(e,e')\rho(e') if e∉SBe\notin S_B, and at most 11 if e∈SBe\in S_B. Let

ρ*=∑k≥0Ck𝟏SB,so thatρ*=𝟏SB+Cρ*. \rho^*=\sum_{k\ge0}C^k\,\mathbf 1_{S_B},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\rho^*=\mathbf 1_{S_B}+C\rho^* .

The sum converges because every row of CC sums to at most α<1\alpha<1. Exactly as in Fact 7.3, the excess max⁡e(ρt(e)−ρ*(e))+\max_e(\rho_t(e)-\rho^*(e))_+ shrinks by a factor α\alpha per sweep. So ρt≤ρ*+αt\rho_t\le\rho^*+\alpha^t.

Step 4 (read off AA). |⟨A⟩ν−⟨A⟩|=|𝔼[A(Yt)−A(Xt)]|≤osc⁡(A)∑e∈SAρt(e)\big|\langle A\rangle_\nu-\langle A\rangle\big|=\big|\mathbb E[A(Y_t)-A(X_t)]\big|\le\operatorname{osc}(A)\sum_{e\in S_A}\rho_t(e). Now let t→∞t\to\infty.

Step 5 (count paths). Ck(e,e′)C^k(e,e') is a sum over chains of kk face-sharing steps from ee to e′e', so it vanishes unless k≥Dk\ge D. Each power satisfies ∑e′Ck(e,e′)≤αk\sum_{e'}C^k(e,e')\le\alpha^k. So ρ*(e)≤∑k≥Dαk=αD/(1−α)\rho^*(e)\le\sum_{k\ge D}\alpha^k=\alpha^D/(1-\alpha) for e∈SAe\in S_A. ▫\square

Notice what the proof never used: reframings, the group structure, or the dimension. Only the sparsity of who-listens-to-whom (a bounded-degree influence graph) and the weakness of each link matter. (Probabilists call this Dobrushin's uniqueness theorem. Computer scientists know its relatives as "spatial mixing" and "correlation decay", the engine of several deterministic approximate-counting algorithms.)

Checking Fact 7.4 exactly. We need a model where correlations at long distances can be computed exactly, not sampled. The transfer matrix of chapter 5 does that. Take the 3D coin model, layered so that each layer is a 3×33\times3 torus. Its 18 layer edges give 2182^{18} layer labelings, and the invariant functions form a subspace of dimension 1024. The helper below applies TT without ever writing it down, using two facts:

  • with the step edges summed out, a slab reweights each layer edge independently;
  • the invariant functions are spanned by products of labels along even subgraphs of the layer (every vertex touched an even number of times), because for an abelian group invariants are functions of loop products (glossary 3.5).

Its derivation is in the helper's comments.

# --- helpers: the exact transfer matrix of the 3D coin model on a W x W torus layer ----------------
# Layer labeling a in {+1,-1}^n (n = 2 W^2 layer edges). Summing the step edges over all their values is
# the same as summing over reframings of the upper layer (Fact 5.1), so on invariant functions
#     T = 2^(W^2) * D^(1/2) K D^(1/2),   K(a,b) = prod_e exp(-beta (1 - a_e b_e)),
# with D(a) = exp(-beta * total cost of the faces inside the layer) (split half and half between slabs).
# K is a tensor power of a 2x2 matrix, diagonal in the "parity" basis chi_S(a) = prod_{e in S} a_e,
# with eigenvalue (1+q)^(n-|S|) (1-q)^|S|, q = exp(-2 beta). chi_S is invariant exactly when S has even
# degree at every vertex. We apply K with a fast Walsh-Hadamard transform (n passes of sums/differences).

def layer_geometry(W):
    edges = [((x, y), mu) for x in range(W) for y in range(W) for mu in (0, 1)]
    eid = {e: i for i, e in enumerate(edges)}
    head = lambda v, mu: ((v[0] + (mu == 0)) % W, (v[1] + (mu == 1)) % W)
    faces = [[eid[((x, y), 0)], eid[(head((x, y), 0), 1)], eid[(head((x, y), 1), 0)], eid[((x, y), 1)]]
             for x in range(W) for y in range(W)]
    at_vertex = {}
    for (v, mu), i in eid.items():
        at_vertex.setdefault(v, []).append(i); at_vertex.setdefault(head(v, mu), []).append(i)
    return len(edges), faces, at_vertex

def walsh(f, n):
    x = f.reshape((2,) * n)
    for ax in range(n):
        a, b = np.take(x, 0, axis=ax), np.take(x, 1, axis=ax)
        x = np.stack([a + b, a - b], axis=ax)
    return x.reshape(-1)

class CoinLayerTransfer:
    """T / 2^(W^2) restricted to invariant functions of a W x W torus layer of the 3D coin model."""
    def __init__(self, W, beta):
        n, faces, at_vertex = layer_geometry(W)
        idx = np.arange(2 ** n, dtype=np.int64)
        bit = lambda i: np.int64(1) << (n - 1 - i)
        self.label = lambda i: 1 - 2 * ((idx & bit(i)) != 0)        # value of layer edge i, per labeling
        S_in = sum(1 - np.prod([self.label(e) for e in f], axis=0) for f in faces)
        self.Dh, self.n, self.faces = np.exp(-beta * S_in / 2), n, faces
        pop = lambda m: np.bitwise_count((idx & m).astype(np.uint64)).astype(np.int64)
        even = np.ones(2 ** n, dtype=bool)
        for es in at_vertex.values():
            even &= pop(np.bitwise_or.reduce([bit(e) for e in es])) % 2 == 0
        q, size = np.exp(-2 * beta), pop(np.int64(-1) & (2 ** n - 1))
        self.diag = even * (1 + q) ** (n - size) * (1 - q) ** size / 2 ** n
    def apply(self, f):
        return self.Dh * walsh(self.diag * walsh(self.Dh * f, self.n), self.n)
    def top(self, k=2):
        op = LinearOperator((2 ** self.n,) * 2, matvec=self.apply, dtype=float)
        vals, vecs = eigsh(op, k=k, which="LA")
        order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]
        return vals[order], vecs[:, order]

Check yourself

  1. Fact 7.4 has |SA||S_A| but not |SB||S_B| in the bound. Why the asymmetry?
Answer

Disagreements are created on SBS_B, with probability at most 1 per edge. But we bound their arrival at each edge of SAS_A by a sum over all sources, which is already included in ρ*\rho^*. Then we add over the edges of SAS_A that AA reads. (Applying the fact with AA and BB swapped gives the other version; use whichever is smaller.)

  1. Does Fact 7.4 say anything about non-invariant functions, such as a single label?
Answer

Yes. It holds for all functions, but for a single label it is vacuous: single labels average to zero (chapter 3), and so do their products at different edges. The real content is about invariants such as face products and loop observables.

7.4 From decorrelation to a gap

Chapter 5 showed, by experiment, that the gap is the rate at which layers forget each other. Now we can run that observation backwards as a proof. Decorrelation at rate α\alpha per layer forces every level to sit at least ln⁡(1/α)\ln(1/\alpha) above the lowest.

Fact 7.5 (a provable gap). Take a layered torus grid in dd dimensions whose layers are (d−1)(d-1)-dimensional tori of side W≥3W\ge3. Let GG be any compact group and φ\varphi any face cost of spread Φ\Phi. If α=6(d−1)tanh⁡(βΦ/2)<1\alpha=6(d-1)\tanh(\beta\Phi/2)<1, then every level of the transfer matrix satisfies En≥ln⁡(1/α)E_n\ge\ln(1/\alpha) for n≥1n\ge1. In particular the gap is at least ln⁡(1/α)\ln(1/\alpha), for every width WW.

Proof. We write it for finite GG. For compact GG, replace sums by integrals; the Perron–Frobenius theorem for matrices is then replaced by its version for positive integral kernels (Jentzsch's theorem).

Step 1 (the top eigenvector is positive). Every entry T(a,b)T(a,b) is a sum of positive weights, and TT is symmetric (chapter 5). By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, λ0\lambda_0 is simple and has an eigenvector ψ0\psi_0 with every entry positive.

Step 2 (a test function for each level). Let ψn\psi_n be a unit eigenvector for λn\lambda_n, n≥1n\ge1, so ψn⟂ψ0\psi_n\perp\psi_0. Define the function of a layer labeling F=ψn/ψ0F=\psi_n/\psi_0; this is legal because ψ0>0\psi_0>0. On a board with NN layers, the average of F(a0)F(at)F(a_0)F(a_t) is tr⁡(MFTtMFTN−t)/tr⁡TN\operatorname{tr}(M_FT^tM_FT^{N-t})/\operatorname{tr}T^N. As N→∞N\to\infty, TN−t/λ0N−tT^{N-t}/\lambda_0^{N-t} tends to ψ0ψ0⊤\psi_0\psi_0^\top. So the average tends to

ψ0⊤MFTtMFψ0λ0t=ψn⊤Ttψnλ0t=(λnλ0)t, \frac{\psi_0^\top M_FT^tM_F\psi_0}{\lambda_0^t}=\frac{\psi_n^\top T^t\psi_n}{\lambda_0^t}=\Big(\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_0}\Big)^t,

and ⟨F⟩→ψ0⊤ψn=0\langle F\rangle\to\psi_0^\top\psi_n=0.

Step 3 (apply Fact 7.4). F(a0)F(a_0) reads the (d−1)Wd−1(d-1)W^{d-1} layer edges of layer 0, and F(at)F(a_t) those of layer tt. A face touches at most two consecutive layers, so the influence distance between layer 0 and layer tt is at least min⁡(t,N−t)\min(t,N-t). Fact 7.4 gives, uniformly in NN,

|⟨F(a0)F(at)⟩−⟨F⟩2|≤Kαmin⁡(t,N−t),K=osc⁡(F)2(d−1)Wd−1/(1−α). \big|\langle F(a_0)F(a_t)\rangle-\langle F\rangle^2\big|\le K\alpha^{\min(t,N-t)},\qquad K=\operatorname{osc}(F)^2\,(d-1)W^{d-1}/(1-\alpha).

Step 4 (take roots). Let N→∞N\to\infty: (λn/λ0)t≤Kαt(\lambda_n/\lambda_0)^t\le K\alpha^t for every tt. Take tt-th roots and let t→∞t\to\infty: λn/λ0≤α\lambda_n/\lambda_0\le\alpha. ▫\square

Step 4 is where the width disappears. KK grows with WW, but it is a constant in tt, and the tt-th root kills it. The rate α\alpha only knows about one edge and its 6(d−1)6(d-1) neighbors.

7.5 Why this does not prove the Millennium gap

Fact 7.5 is a real theorem about lattice Yang–Mills. With G=SU(N)G=SU(N) on a 4D grid it is the small-β\beta entry "positive gap at small β\beta, proved" in chapter 6's status table, re-derived with a computer-science tool. Here is how far it reaches:

Model (4D) Spread Φ\Phi Fact 7.5 applies for our β<\beta< physicists' βphys<\beta_{\text{phys}}< Where continuum physics is simulated (βphys\beta_{\text{phys}})
coin 2 0.056 – –
SU(2)SU(2) 4 0.028 0.056 2.3 – 3
SU(3)SU(3) 4.5 0.025 0.074 5.7 – 7

The gap between the last two columns is a factor of 40 to 100. But the true obstruction is not the size of the constants. There are three separate reasons, in increasing order of depth.

1. Influence grows with stiffness. At large β\beta each edge listens strongly to its neighbors, so α>1\alpha>1. This is not only a weakness of the bound: as correlations reach further, the heat-bath sampler of chapter 4 really does slow down, because forgetting in space and forgetting in time go together. Sharper tools from computer science ("spectral independence", block dynamics, "high-dimensional expanders") push the threshold further. But they all certify the same kind of thing: correlations die within a bounded number of grid steps, with constants fixed in advance. Reason 3 shows why that kind of certificate must fail along the continuum path.

2. The board's geometry is not the issue. The hint "use expander graphs" points at a real principle, local-to-global: a local condition on a sparse graph (α<1\alpha<1 on the influence graph) gives a global conclusion. But Fact 7.5 already works on the 4D grid, which is a poor expander: a box of side ℓ\ell has ℓ4\ell^4 vertices but only about ℓ3\ell^3 boundary edges. Expansion of the board plays no role. What matters is the weakness of each link, and that is exactly what fails at large β\beta.

3. The scale problem (the deep one). The Millennium gap is not "the gap is positive". From chapter 6: as the grid spacing ε→0\varepsilon\to0, with β(ε)\beta(\varepsilon) growing like 116π2ln⁡(1/ε)\tfrac{11}{6\pi^2}\ln(1/\varepsilon) for SU(2)SU(2), one needs

E1(β(ε))ε→Δ,0<Δ<∞. \frac{E_1(\beta(\varepsilon))}{\varepsilon}\ \longrightarrow\ \Delta,\qquad 0<\Delta<\infty .

A bound like E1≥ln⁡(1/α)E_1\ge\ln(1/\alpha), a constant in grid units, would give E1/ε→∞E_1/\varepsilon\to\infty if it held along the continuum path: every particle infinitely heavy, and the limit would be trivial. So a proof must show that the gap in grid units shrinks, exponentially in β\beta, at the rate set by asymptotic freedom, and no faster. That is a statement connecting all scales at once. No single-scale argument can deliver it.

What a proof would need, in our terms. Group the grid into blocks of side 2k2^k. The distribution of suitable block labels is again a model, with a more complicated but still local cost. Asymptotic freedom says its effective stiffness decreases as the blocks grow. So the natural plan, Balaban's, is:

  • (UV) Control the blocking from spacing ε\varepsilon up to a fixed physical block size ℓ\ell, uniformly as ε→0\varepsilon\to0. This is what the 2026 proof does, up to a reference scale where the stiffness equals a fixed βref\beta_{\text{ref}} (see notes/yang_mills_existence_2026.md).
  • (Crossover) Keep blocking until the effective stiffness falls below the threshold of a Fact-7.5-type theorem, while keeping the effective cost local.
  • (IR) Apply a Fact-7.5-type theorem, generalized to local costs on several faces (the proof goes through verbatim: influence is still a sum over shared terms of tanh⁡(spread/2)\tanh(\text{spread}/2)), to the blocked model. Then show that fine-scale observables, given the block labels, are themselves local. The result would be a gap of at least ln⁡(1/αeff)/ℓ\ln(1/\alpha_{\text{eff}})/\ell in physical units.

The first step is done. The last is this chapter, plus a locality lemma. The middle step is the open problem. The UV methods work by expanding around consistent labelings, which needs large stiffness. The IR method needs stiffness below about 0.03. Between them is the smooth crossover you saw in chapter 6's Creutz plot, where the model is neither nearly consistent nor nearly independent, and no known method controls it. The 2026 proof itself avoids this region. Per the committee's assessment, its large-volume step rests on compactness, flip-positivity and the transfer-matrix representation, and it proves no clustering of correlations. That is why it obtains existence along a subsequence, a mixture of phases, and no gap.

Recap. Fact 7.5 covers only β\beta below a few hundredths, far from where the continuum lives. Beyond that, influence is too strong. More fundamentally, the Millennium gap needs the grid-unit gap to shrink at exactly the asymptotic-freedom rate, which is a multi-scale statement. The missing piece is control of the crossover between the near-consistent regime (handled by the 2026 existence proof) and the near-independent regime (handled by this chapter).

Chapter recap

  • The influence C(e,e′)C(e,e') is the most the heat-bath distribution at ee can move when the label at e′e' changes. The influence number α\alpha is the largest total influence on one edge. On a grid, α≤6(d−1)tanh⁡(βΦ/2)\alpha\le6(d-1)\tanh(\beta\Phi/2) (Facts 7.1, 7.2).
  • A coupling runs two random processes on shared randomness. With best couplings, two heat-bath samplers disagree at any edge with probability at most αt\alpha^t after tt sweeps (Fact 7.3). This is the path-coupling argument for rapid mixing.
  • Reweighting by BB changes the model only on BB's edges. Coupling the two models gives correlations at most osc⁡(A)osc⁡(B)|SA|αD/(1−α)\operatorname{osc}(A)\operatorname{osc}(B)|S_A|\alpha^D/(1-\alpha) (Fact 7.4).
  • Testing with F=ψn/ψ0F=\psi_n/\psi_0 turns decorrelation into a floor under every level: En≥ln⁡(1/α)E_n\ge\ln(1/\alpha) for every width, every compact group, every dimension (Fact 7.5). Checked against exact 2D and 3D coin-model gaps.
  • For 4D SU(2)SU(2) the theorem applies for β<0.028\beta<0.028. The continuum needs β→∞\beta\to\infty with a gap that shrinks at the asymptotic-freedom rate, a multi-scale statement no single-scale argument can give.
  • The missing step is the crossover between the regime the 2026 existence proof controls and the regime this chapter controls.
