Chapter 3: What a model can tell you

In Chapter 2 you built a model: a probability distribution μβ\mu_\beta on the labelings of a board. A model answers questions, and this chapter is about which questions are worth asking.

Here is a puzzle to start. Take the coin model (G={+1,−1}G=\lbrace +1,-1\rbrace) on a small grid and turn the stiffness up to β=5\beta = 5. Now almost every face is calm (not a defect): each face product is +1+1 with probability 0.999950.99995. The board looks frozen. Surely the edge labels have "settled" on some values? So here is the question: what is the average label on one edge? Make a guess before you scroll down.

What you'll learn

  • How to ask a model a question: the average ⟨f⟩β\langle f\rangle_\beta of a quantity ff.
  • Why a single edge label averages to exactly zero at every stiffness, and why this means a model only ever "knows" invariants.
  • How loop observables behave on a grid (they decay with area), and why loop observables are enough to pin down a class.

Prerequisites:

  • Chapter 1: labelings, walk and loop products, reframings, classes, invariants, class functions, loop observables.
  • Chapter 2: face cost, total cost, stiffness, models, exact enumeration.

1. Asking a model a question: averages

A model is a probability distribution on labelings. A question is any function of the labeling, and the model answers it with an average.

Definition (average, glossary 4.6). Take a model μβ\mu_\beta on a board XX with label group GG. A quantity is any function f:GE→ℝf: G^E\to\mathbb R. Its average is the number

⟨f⟩β=∑U∈GEμβ(U)f(U)∈ℝ(finite G). \langle f\rangle_\beta \;=\; \sum_{U\in G^E}\mu_\beta(U)\,f(U)\;\in\mathbb R \qquad\text{(finite }G\text{)}.

For a continuous group like the circle, the sum becomes an integral against μβ\mu_\beta. Recall from Chapter 2 that for finite GG:

μβ(U)=e−βS(U)∑U′∈GEe−βS(U′). \mu_\beta(U)=\frac{e^{-\beta S(U)}}{\sum_{U'\in G^E}e^{-\beta S(U')}}.

The map f↦⟨f⟩βf\mapsto\langle f\rangle_\beta is linear: ⟨af+bg⟩β=a⟨f⟩β+b⟨g⟩β\langle af+bg\rangle_\beta=a\langle f\rangle_\beta+b\langle g\rangle_\beta.

Example. Take the coin model on a 3×33\times 3 grid of vertices, which has 12 edges and 4 faces. It has 212=40962^{12}=4096 labelings, few enough to list them all. Model.exact() does exactly that, and .average(f) computes the weighted sum. We ask two questions:

  • What is the total cost SS on average?
  • What is the face product UfU_f of the bottom-left face on average?
B = Board.grid(3, 3)

Fact 3.1 (single-edge labels are uniform). Take any model: any board, any compact label group GG, any face cost, any stiffness β\beta. For every edge ee whose two endpoints are different, the label U(e)U(e) is uniformly distributed on GG. In particular:

  • ⟨U(e)⟩β=0\langle U(e)\rangle_\beta=0 for the coin model;
  • ⟨cos⁡U(e)⟩β=⟨sin⁡U(e)⟩β=0\langle\cos U(e)\rangle_\beta=\langle\sin U(e)\rangle_\beta=0 for circle labels.

Why. Write ee as s→ts\to t and pick any g∈Gg\in G. Use the reframing hh with h(s)=gh(s)=g and h(v)=1h(v)=1 at every other vertex.

  • It changes U(e)U(e) to gU(e)g\,U(e).
  • It leaves μβ\mu_\beta unchanged. The total cost is an invariant, and the uniform distribution doesn't change when labels are multiplied by gg.

So U(e)U(e) and gU(e)gU(e) have the same distribution, for every gg. The only distribution on GG that is unchanged by every multiplication is the uniform one.

So high stiffness calms the faces, but it says nothing about individual edges. Every reframing of the all-identity labeling has no defects, so they all cost the same, and the model spreads its probability evenly over all of them.

Recap. A quantity that a reframing can change, like a single edge label, gets smeared out by the model. Its distribution is uniform at every β\beta, so its average carries no information.

Check yourself

  1. In a model with G=S3G=S_3 (permutations of three things), what is the probability that U(e)=(12)U(e)=(12)?
Answer

It is 1/61/6, the same as for every other element, at every β\beta (Fact 3.1).

  1. Take an open walk γ\gamma from ss to tt with s≠ts\neq t. Is its walk product U(γ)U(\gamma) uniform too?
Answer

Yes. A reframing at ss alone multiplies U(γ)U(\gamma) on the left by h(s)h(s), because the inner factors cancel (Chapter 1). Only closed walks escape this argument, because their two ends are the same vertex.

  1. Why did the fact need the endpoints of ee to be different?
Answer

If ee goes from vv back to vv, a reframing changes its label to h(v)U(e)h(v)−1h(v)\,U(e)\,h(v)^{-1}, a conjugate. Its conjugacy class can't be changed this way, so its label need not be uniform. Such an edge is itself a closed walk.

3. What a model really knows: a distribution on classes

Fact 3.1 is one instance of a general rule: averaging over reframings never changes an average.

Fact 3.2 (averages only see invariants). For a quantity ff, define its reframing average f‾:GE→ℝ\bar f:G^E\to\mathbb R by letting f‾(U)\bar f(U) be the average of f(reframed U)f(\text{reframed }U) over a uniformly random reframing hh. For finite GG this is |G|−|V|∑hf(reframed U)|G|^{-|V|}\sum_{h}f(\text{reframed }U). Then f‾\bar f is an invariant, and ⟨f⟩β=⟨f‾⟩β\langle f\rangle_\beta=\langle\bar f\rangle_\beta for every β\beta.

Why. Every reframing leaves μβ\mu_\beta unchanged, so replacing f(U)f(U) by f(reframed U)f(\text{reframed }U) doesn't change its average. Averaging over all reframings doesn't change it either.

We check this on the coin model on the 2×22\times2 torus, which has 4 vertices, 8 edges and 4 faces. The quantity is deliberately not an invariant: "edge 0 is +1+1 and face 0 is calm."

T = Board.grid(2, 2, periodic=True)
exT = Model(T, G, beta=1.0).exact()
H = all_reframings(G, T)                                    # all 2^4 = 16 reframings

def f(U):           # not an invariant
    return ((U[..., 0] == 1) & (face_products(G, T, U)[..., 0] == 1)).astype(float)

def f_bar(U):       # its reframing average
    return np.mean([f(reframe(G, T, U, h)) for h in H], axis=0)

print("<f> =", round(exT.average(f), 6), "   <f_bar> =", round(exT.average(f_bar), 6))
print("f_bar is an invariant:", all(np.allclose(f_bar(exT.U), f_bar(reframe(G, T, exT.U, h))) for h in H))
print("f is an invariant:    ", all(np.allclose(f(exT.U), f(reframe(G, T, exT.U, h))) for h in H))

So everything a model can tell you comes from the probabilities it gives to classes. Each class has the same number of members (Chapter 2), so the probability of a class [U][U] is proportional to e−βS(U)e^{-\beta S(U)}.

At high stiffness, nearly all the probability sits on four classes with no defects, tied with each other. Why four? On a board that wraps around, some loops aren't built out of faces:

  • the loop that goes once around horizontally;
  • the loop that goes once around vertically.

Their loop products are invariants that the faces can't see.

  • The loop "around y" is the left column.

Recap. Averages only see invariants (Fact 3.2). A model is therefore, in effect, a probability distribution on classes, and the probability of a class is proportional to e−βSe^{-\beta S}. Some invariants (like the loop products around a torus) are invisible to the faces.

4. Loop observables on a grid: they decay with area

Recall from Chapter 1 (glossary 3.7) the loop observable of a closed walk γ\gamma: Wγ(U)=χ(U(γ))W_\gamma(U)=\chi\big(U(\gamma)\big), where χ:G→ℂ\chi:G\to\mathbb C is a class function and Wγ:GE→ℂW_\gamma:G^E\to\mathbb C. It is an invariant, so the model has something to say about it. For the coin model, χ(x)=x\chi(x)=x, so Wγ=U(γ)∈{+1,−1}W_\gamma=U(\gamma)\in\lbrace+1,-1\rbrace is real.

Here is the question. On a big planar grid, take γ\gamma to be the boundary of an a×ba\times b rectangle, walked counterclockwise. How does ⟨Wγ⟩β\langle W_\gamma\rangle_\beta depend on the rectangle's shape?

Fact 3.3 (a loop is the product of the faces inside). On a planar grid with an abelian label group, the loop product around a rectangle equals the product of the face products of the faces inside it.

Why. Multiply the face products of all the faces inside. Every interior edge is walked once in each direction, by the two faces on either side of it, so its label cancels. Only the boundary is left.

We check this on a random labeling of a 6×66\times6 grid of vertices (5 × 5 faces):

B6 = Board.grid(6, 6)
face_at = {c: i for i, (c, mu, nu) in enumerate(B6.meta["face_anchor"])}

def rect_walk(a, b, L=6):
    """Vertices around the a-by-b rectangle with corner at the origin, counterclockwise."""
    pts = ([(i, 0) for i in range(a)] + [(a, j) for j in range(b)]
           + [(a - i, b) for i in range(a)] + [(0, b - j) for j in range(b + 1)])
    return [x + L * y for x, y in pts]

U = random_labeling(G, B6, rng)

Combine Fact 3.3 with Chapter 2's fact that, on a planar grid, the face products are independent, each with average tanh⁡β\tanh\beta. The average of a product of independent variables is the product of their averages, so:

⟨Wa×b⟩β=(tanh⁡β)ab,log⁡⟨Wa×b⟩β=ab⋅log⁡tanh⁡β. \langle W_{a\times b}\rangle_\beta=(\tanh\beta)^{ab},\qquad \log\langle W_{a\times b}\rangle_\beta = ab\cdot\log\tanh\beta .

The prediction is that the average depends on the area abab only, not on the perimeter 2(a+b)2(a+b).

To test it without leaning on the formula, we measure loop products on samples of the model. A 6×66\times6 grid has 60 edges, far too many labelings to enumerate. But Chapter 2's independence fact gives an exact sampler:

  1. Draw every face product independently: +1+1 with probability 1/(1+e−2β)1/(1+e^{-2\beta}).
  2. Build a labeling with those face products, in tree normal form for a comb-shaped spanning tree (all vertical edges plus the bottom row). Each remaining edge sits directly on top of exactly one face, so its label is fixed by that face's product and the edge below it.
  3. Apply a uniformly random reframing. Within its class, the model is uniform.
def sample_coin_planar(board, beta, n, rng, reframe_after=True):
    """Exact samples of the coin model on a planar grid. Returns (labelings, face products)."""
    Lx, Ly = board.meta["dims"]; eidx = board.meta["edge_index"]
    fidx = {c: i for i, (c, mu, nu) in enumerate(board.meta["face_anchor"])}
    faces = np.where(rng.random((n, board.n_faces)) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * beta)), 1, -1).astype(np.int8)
    U = np.ones((n, board.n_edges), dtype=np.int8)         # comb tree edges stay +1
    for y in range(Ly - 1):
        for x in range(Lx - 1):
            below, above = eidx[(x + Lx * y, 0)], eidx[(x + Lx * (y + 1), 0)]
            U[:, above] = faces[:, fidx[(x, y)]] * U[:, below]
    if reframe_after:
        U = reframe(Z2(), board, U, Z2().uniform(rng, (n, board.n_vertices)))
    return U, faces

comb = [e for (v, mu), e in B6.meta["edge_index"].items() if mu == 1 or v < 6]
U_comb, faces = sample_coin_planar(B6, 1.0, 1, rng, reframe_after=False)
print("face products came out as drawn:", np.array_equal(face_products(G, B6, U_comb), faces))

  1. Where did the proof of Fact 3.3 use that GG is abelian?
Answer

The proof cancels each interior edge's label against its inverse. When GG is non-abelian, the two occurrences aren't next to each other in the product. Face products must first be carried to a common starting vertex (which conjugates them), and only then multiplied together.

5. Non-abelian labels: what the class function sees

For a non-abelian group, a reframing changes a loop product to a conjugate, h(v)U(γ)h(v)−1h(v)\,U(\gamma)\,h(v)^{-1} (Chapter 1). A class function χ\chi can't tell conjugates apart, so χ(U(γ))\chi(U(\gamma)) is an invariant.

Take G=S3G=S_3, the permutations of three things, with χ:S3→ℝ\chi:S_3\to\mathbb R, χ(x)=\chi(x)= the number of points that xx leaves fixed. This is the trace of xx's 3×33\times3 permutation matrix.

  • The identity has χ=3\chi=3.
  • The three swaps have χ=1\chi=1.
  • The two 3-cycles have χ=0\chi=0.

The board is a 2×22\times2 grid: one face and four edges. Below, the same labeling is put through five random reframings:

6. Loop observables pin down the class

Section 3 showed that a model is a distribution on classes. Sections 4 and 5 measured loop observables. Are loop observables enough to tell any two classes apart?

Fact 3.4 (loop observables determine the class). Two labelings of a connected board are in the same class exactly when all of their loop observables agree.

  • For an abelian group where labels are numbers (ℤ2\mathbb Z_2, ℤN\mathbb Z_N, circle angles), the loop products themselves serve as the observables.
  • For matrix groups, take χ\chi to be the trace itself, not just its real part. For S3S_3, the number of fixed points works; we check this on an example below.

Why (abelian case). The loop products of the fundamental cycles are exactly the labels that remain in tree normal form (Chapter 1), and the tree normal form identifies the class. The non-abelian case is harder, so we verify it by brute force instead.

The board for this check is a 3×23\times2 grid of vertices: 6 vertices, 7 edges and 2 faces. With S3S_3 labels it has 67=279,9366^7=279{,}936 labelings. We compute the class of every labeling, and the loop observables of a handful of closed walks at vertex 0. Let aa be the left face and bb the right face, both walked from vertex 0. Words like abab mean "walk aa, then bb"; a capital letter means "walk it backwards".

7. Correlations between invariants

Definition (correlation, glossary 4.7). The correlation of two quantities f,g:GE→ℝf,g:G^E\to\mathbb R is

corr⁡β(f,g)=⟨fg⟩β−⟨f⟩β⟨g⟩β∈ℝ. \operatorname{corr}_\beta(f,g)=\langle fg\rangle_\beta-\langle f\rangle_\beta\langle g\rangle_\beta\;\in\mathbb R.

If ff and gg are independent, the correlation is 00. Suppose correlations between quantities at distance dd shrink like e−d/ξe^{-d/\xi} for large dd. Then the number ξ>0\xi>0 is called the correlation length: it is how far apart two parts of the board must be before they stop "talking".

On a planar grid there is nothing to measure. Below are the exact correlations between the four face products of the 3×33\times3 grid, at β=1\beta=1:

def face_corr(board, beta):
    ex = Model(board, G, beta).exact()
    Fp = face_products(G, board, ex.U).astype(float)
    mean = ex.p @ Fp
    return (Fp * ex.p[:, None]).T @ Fp - np.outer(mean, mean)

print("\n3x3 torus (9 faces), first row:\n", np.round(face_corr(Board.grid(3, 3, periodic=True), 1.0)[0], 6))

What to look at:

  • Planar grid: the off-diagonal correlations are exactly 00, because the faces are independent. The diagonal is the variance 1−tanh⁡2β1-\tanh^2\beta.
  • Torus: there is a small correlation, the same for every pair of faces, whether they are neighbours or not. It comes from a single global constraint: the product of all face products is +1+1. Nothing passes from face to face.

So the boards we have seen have no interesting correlation length. Later chapters use boards that are stacked in layers, or that fill three or four dimensions. There, invariants in different places do influence each other, correlations decay with distance, and the correlation length becomes one of the most important numbers in the course.

Recap. corr⁡β(f,g)=⟨fg⟩−⟨f⟩⟨g⟩\operatorname{corr}_\beta(f,g)=\langle fg\rangle-\langle f\rangle\langle g\rangle. On planar grids, distinct faces are uncorrelated. On a small torus there is a uniform, distance-free correlation from one global constraint.

Check yourself

  1. Why is the product of all face products +1+1 on the torus?
Answer

Every edge lies on exactly two faces, which walk it in opposite directions. So each label appears twice in the product of all face products, as U(e)U(e) and U(e)−1=U(e)U(e)^{-1}=U(e), and (±1)2=1(\pm1)^2=1.

Chapter recap

  • A question to a model is a quantity f:GE→ℝf:G^E\to\mathbb R. The answer is the average ⟨f⟩β\langle f\rangle_\beta.
  • Fact 3.1: a single edge label is uniformly distributed at every stiffness. High stiffness calms faces, but never "decides" edges.
  • Fact 3.2: averages only see invariants. A model is, in effect, a distribution on classes, with probability proportional to e−βSe^{-\beta S}.
  • Fact 3.3: on planar grids with abelian labels, a rectangle's loop product is the product of the faces inside it. So ⟨W⟩β=(tanh⁡β)area\langle W\rangle_\beta=(\tanh\beta)^{\text{area}} for the coin model.
  • With non-abelian labels, class functions such as the trace turn loop products into invariants.
  • Fact 3.4: loop observables determine the class. Faces alone are not enough: you also need loops that go around several faces, or around the board.
  • Correlations between faces vanish on planar grids. Correlation length is a story for layered and higher-dimensional boards.
