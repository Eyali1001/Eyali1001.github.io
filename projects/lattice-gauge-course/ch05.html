Chapter 5 — Layers and the transfer matrix: dynamics from a distribution

So far a model has been a single, static object: one probability distribution over whole labelings of a board. Nothing in it "happens". There is no before and after.

Now take a board shaped like a stack of rings: a cylinder. Cut it into its rings, one layer at a time, and ask a modest question: if I know the labels on one ring, what do I know about the next one? The answer turns out to be one matrix. Its eigenvalues are numbers you can also measure by sampling the model and watching how quickly two rings forget each other. And on the simplest boards, the ring's loop product does nothing more than a random walk on the group.

This chapter is the bridge of the course. On one side is "a model is a distribution". On the other side is a vector space with an operator acting on it, which physicists would recognize as a quantum system. We will build that operator from the distribution, never assume it.

What you'll learn

  • How cutting a board into layers turns a model into a chain of matrix multiplications (the transfer matrix).
  • Why, on a cylinder, the loop product of a layer performs a random walk on GG, and what the eigenvalues of that walk mean.
  • How the same numbers come out of sampling the model as a black box: two roads to one number.

Prerequisites: chapters 1–4. From linear algebra: eigenvalues of symmetric matrices. From probability: Markov chains.

5.1 Cutting a board into layers

Take a grid with WW columns and NN rows and wrap it around in both directions. Each row is now a ring of WW vertices. We call the rows layers V0,V1,…,VN−1V_0,V_1,\dots,V_{N-1}, with layer indices taken mod NN, so layer NN is layer 00 again (glossary 5.2).

Every edge is one of two kinds:

  • a layer edge joins two vertices of the same layer (the edges going around a ring);
  • a step edge joins a vertex of layer kk to the vertex above it in layer k+1k+1.

Every face is a square with two layer edges (one in layer kk, one in layer k+1k+1) and two step edges. So the faces sit between consecutive layers, in a band we call a slab. A layer labeling (glossary 5.5) is the restriction of a labeling UU to the WW layer edges of one layer: an element a∈Ω:=GWa\in\Omega:=G^{W}.

If you ignore the wrap-around in the vertical direction, the board looks like a cylinder: rings stacked on top of each other, joined by step edges. Below, one layer is drawn in teal and the step edges above it in gold. (Time wraps around too, so the top ring is glued back to the bottom one.)

Symmetries. A symmetry of a board (glossary 5.1) is a bijection of the vertices that maps edges to edges and faces to faces. The total cost only looks at face products, so a symmetry of the board is automatically a symmetry of every model on it. Our board has two that matter:

  • the shift τ\tau moves every vertex up one layer, (x,k)↦(x,k+1)(x,k)\mapsto(x,k+1) (glossary 5.3);
  • the flip θ\theta turns the stack upside down, (x,k)↦(x,−k)(x,k)\mapsto(x,-k) (glossary 5.4).

Let's check both with a few lines of code, together with a bijection that is not a symmetry.

def is_symmetry(board, vmap):
    edges = lambda m: sorted(tuple(sorted((m[s], m[t]))) for s, t in board.edges.tolist())
    faces = lambda m: sorted(tuple(sorted({m[v] for v in board.walk_vertices(f)})) for f in board.faces)
    ident = list(range(board.n_vertices))
    return edges(vmap) == edges(ident) and faces(vmap) == faces(ident)

W, N = 4, 6
B = Board.grid(W, N, periodic=True)                    # vertex (x, k) has index x + W*k
vid = lambda x, k: (x % W) + W * (k % N)
shift = [vid(x, k + 1) for k in range(N) for x in range(W)]
flip = [vid(x, -k) for k in range(N) for x in range(W)]
twist = [vid(-x if k == 0 else x, k) for k in range(N) for x in range(W)]  # mirror layer 0 only

shift: True   flip: True   twist: False

Why do we care? Because of the shift, every slab looks exactly like every other slab. Whatever rule tells us how layer kk constrains layer k+1k+1, it is the same rule for every kk, just like a time-homogeneous Markov chain uses the same transition matrix at every step. The flip will give that rule a special shape (sections 5.4 and 5.6).

Recap. A layered board splits its vertices into layers V0,…,VN−1V_0,\dots,V_{N-1}. Layer edges stay inside a layer, step edges go from one layer to the next, and every face lives in the slab between two consecutive layers. The shift and the flip are symmetries of the board, hence of every model on it.

5.2 The transfer matrix

This section builds one matrix, TT, that contains the whole model on the stack. We get there in three small steps: a toy chain, one slab of our board, and then the matrix itself.

Warm-up: a sum over sequences is a matrix power

Forget boards for a moment. Suppose you have a finite set of states Ω\Omega and a table of nonnegative weights K(a,b)K(a,b), one for every ordered pair of states. Give a sequence of states a0,a1,…,ata_0,a_1,\dots,a_t the weight

K(a0,a1)K(a1,a2)⋯K(at−1,at). K(a_0,a_1)\,K(a_1,a_2)\cdots K(a_{t-1},a_t).

If KK were a table of transition probabilities, this would be the probability of a path of a Markov chain. Here it is just a weight. Now fix the two ends a0a_0 and ata_t and add up the weights of all sequences in between. For t=2t=2 this sum is

∑a1∈ΩK(a0,a1)K(a1,a2), \sum_{a_1\in\Omega}K(a_0,a_1)\,K(a_1,a_2),

which is exactly the (a0,a2)(a_0,a_2) entry of the matrix product K⋅KK\cdot K. By the same reasoning, summing over all the states in between, for sequences of length tt, gives the entry (Kt)(a0,at)(K^t)(a_0,a_t) of the tt-th matrix power. This is the same fact you use when you compute tt-step probabilities of a Markov chain, or count walks of length tt in a graph with powers of its adjacency matrix.

One more twist. If the sequence is cyclic, meaning it must end where it started (at=a0a_t=a_0), then adding up over a0a_0 as well gives ∑a0(Kt)(a0,a0)\sum_{a_0}(K^t)(a_0,a_0), the trace tr⁡Kt\operatorname{tr}K^t. Let's check both statements by brute force on a two-state example.

Matrix powers are a compact way to add up exponentially many products. Now we show that our model on the stack is a weighted sequence of this kind, with states = layer labelings.

One slab

Look at the slab between layer kk and layer k+1k+1 of the W×NW\times N torus. It contains:

  • the WW layer edges of layer kk, carrying the layer labeling a=(a0,…,aW−1)∈Ω=GWa=(a_0,\dots,a_{W-1})\in\Omega=G^W;
  • the WW layer edges of layer k+1k+1, carrying b=(b0,…,bW−1)∈Ωb=(b_0,\dots,b_{W-1})\in\Omega;
  • the WW step edges between them, carrying s=(s0,…,sW−1)∈GWs=(s_0,\dots,s_{W-1})\in G^W;
  • WW faces. Face xx is the square between columns xx and x+1x+1; its four edges are axa_x, bxb_x, sxs_x and sx+1s_{x+1} (with sW=s0s_W=s_0, since the ring closes).

Every face of the board lies in exactly one slab, and a face in this slab involves only aa, bb and ss. So the total cost splits into slab costs, and the weight e−βS(U)e^{-\beta S(U)} splits into a product of slab weights:

e−βS(U)=∏k=0N−1q(ak,sk,ak+1),q(a,s,b)=∏x=0W−1e−βφ(face x)∈ℝ>0. e^{-\beta S(U)}=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1} q(a_k,s_k,a_{k+1}),\qquad q(a,s,b)=\prod_{x=0}^{W-1}e^{-\beta\varphi(\text{face }x)}\ \in\mathbb R_{>0}.

Here q(a,s,b)q(a,s,b) is the slab weight: the weight of one slab whose lower layer carries aa, whose step edges carry ss and whose upper layer carries bb. On the left, ak∈Ωa_k\in\Omega is the whole layer labeling of layer kk and sk∈GWs_k\in G^W the step labels of slab kk. So from now on the subscript tells you what is meant: axa_x with a column index xx is one label of a layer labeling aa; aka_k (or ata_t) with a layer index is the layer labeling of layer kk.

For the coin model (G=ℤ2G=\mathbb Z_2, labels ±1\pm1, which are their own inverses) the face product of face xx is simply axsx+1bxsxa_x\,s_{x+1}\,b_x\,s_x, and φ(f)=1−f\varphi(f)=1-f.

The matrix

The step labels ss of a slab appear in that slab's weight and nowhere else. So we can add them up slab by slab, which leaves a weight that depends only on the two neighboring layer labelings. That is the transfer matrix (glossary 5.6):

T(a,b)=∑s∈GWq(a,s,b),T∈ℝΩ×Ω. T(a,b)=\sum_{s\in G^W}q(a,s,b),\qquad T\in\mathbb R^{\Omega\times\Omega}.

Its rows and columns are indexed by layer labelings. For the coin model with W=3W=3 there are 23=82^3=8 of them, so TT is 8×88\times8. Let's compute one entry by hand first, T(a,b)T(a,b) with a=b=(+1,+1,+1)a=b=(+1,+1,+1). The face products become sxsx+1s_xs_{x+1}, so each face is a defect exactly when sx≠sx+1s_x\neq s_{x+1}. Going through the 8 choices of ss: 2 of them (all equal) give no defects, and the other 6 give exactly 2 defects. Each defect costs φ(−1)=2\varphi(-1)=2, so

T((+,+,+),(+,+,+))=2⋅1+6⋅e−4β. T\big((+,+,+),(+,+,+)\big)=2\cdot1+6\cdot e^{-4\beta}. 

def layer_labelings(W):
    """All 2^W coin-model layer labelings, as rows of +-1."""
    return np.array(list(itertools.product([1, -1], repeat=W)))

def coin_transfer_matrix(W, beta):
    A = layer_labelings(W)                      # rows of A also serve as the step labels s
    T = np.zeros((len(A), len(A)))
    for i, a in enumerate(A):
        for j, b in enumerate(A):
            f = a * b * A * np.roll(A, -1, axis=1)          # face x: a_x b_x s_x s_{x+1}, one row per s
            T[i, j] = np.exp(-beta * (1 - f)).prod(axis=1).sum()
    return T

The normalizer ZZ is a trace

Recall from chapter 2 what a model needs: a weight e−βS(U)e^{-\beta S(U)} for every labeling, and the total of all weights,

Z=∑Ue−βS(U)∈ℝ>0 Z=\sum_{U}e^{-\beta S(U)}\ \in\mathbb R_{>0}

(for a finite group, summing over all labelings UU). Every probability is a weight divided by ZZ, and every average is a ratio of two such sums. (Chapter 2 divided ZZ by the number of labelings, to compare with the uniform distribution. That changes ZZ by a constant factor, which cancels from every probability.)

Computing ZZ by brute force means visiting all |G||E||G|^{|E|} labelings. The transfer matrix replaces this with a matrix power, in three steps.

Step 1: organize the sum by layers and slabs. A labeling of the stack is the same thing as a list of NN layer labelings a0,…,aN−1a_0,\dots,a_{N-1} together with NN lists of step labels s0,…,sN−1s_0,\dots,s_{N-1}. Its weight is a product of slab weights, with aN=a0a_N=a_0 because the stack wraps around:

Z=∑a0,…,aN−1∑s0,…,sN−1q(a0,s0,a1)q(a1,s1,a2)⋯q(aN−1,sN−1,a0). Z=\sum_{a_0,\dots,a_{N-1}}\ \sum_{s_0,\dots,s_{N-1}}\ q(a_0,s_0,a_1)\,q(a_1,s_1,a_2)\cdots q(a_{N-1},s_{N-1},a_0).

Step 2: sum out the step labels. The labels sks_k appear in factor kk and in no other factor, so their sum can be done inside that factor, and it gives T(ak,ak+1)T(a_k,a_{k+1}) by definition:

Z=∑a0,…,aN−1T(a0,a1)T(a1,a2)⋯T(aN−1,a0). Z=\sum_{a_0,\dots,a_{N-1}}T(a_0,a_1)\,T(a_1,a_2)\cdots T(a_{N-1},a_0).

Step 3: recognize the warm-up. This is a sum over cyclic sequences of states, weighted by K=TK=T. So it is a trace:

Z=tr⁡TN. Z=\operatorname{tr}T^N .

In the smallest case, N=2N=2, you can see it directly: Z=∑a0,a1T(a0,a1)T(a1,a0)=∑a0(T2)(a0,a0)=tr⁡T2Z=\sum_{a_0,a_1}T(a_0,a_1)\,T(a_1,a_0)=\sum_{a_0}(T^2)(a_0,a_0)=\operatorname{tr}T^2.

(On boards of dimension 3 or more, some faces lie inside a layer. Their cost is then split half-and-half between the two slabs touching that layer, so that TT stays symmetric. On our 2D torus every face is in a slab, so no splitting is needed.)

They agree to every digit. A sum over 262,144 labelings has become the trace of the cube of an 8×88\times8 matrix. For a taller board we would only raise TT to a higher power, and the matrix stays 8×88\times8.

Why a trace? Because the stack is periodic: layer NN is layer 00, so the sequence of layers must return to where it started. If instead the stack had two separate ends, with the bottom layer fixed to aa and the top layer fixed to bb, the same three steps would give a single matrix entry, (TN)(a,b)(T^N)(a,b). The trace is the periodic version of that.

Averages are traces too

The same bookkeeping computes averages. Suppose f:Ω→ℝf:\Omega\to\mathbb R is a function of one layer's labeling, and you want ⟨f(a0)⟩β\langle f(a_0)\rangle_\beta. An average is the sum of (weight × ff) divided by ZZ, so put the factor f(a0)f(a_0) into the sum of Step 2. In matrix language, let Df∈ℝΩ×ΩD_f\in\mathbb R^{\Omega\times\Omega} be the diagonal matrix with the values f(a)f(a) on its diagonal. Then (DfT)(a0,a1)=f(a0)T(a0,a1)(D_fT)(a_0,a_1)=f(a_0)\,T(a_0,a_1), so multiplying by f(a0)f(a_0) is the same as inserting DfD_f just before the first TT:

∑a0,…,aN−1f(a0)T(a0,a1)⋯T(aN−1,a0)=tr⁡(DfTN). \sum_{a_0,\dots,a_{N-1}}f(a_0)\,T(a_0,a_1)\cdots T(a_{N-1},a_0)=\operatorname{tr}\big(D_f\,T^N\big).

A second function gg of layer tt (for 0≤t≤N0\le t\le N) is inserted the same way, as DgD_g after the first tt factors of TT. Dividing by Z=tr⁡TNZ=\operatorname{tr}T^N gives:

Rule (averages as traces). For functions f,g:Ω→ℝf,g:\Omega\to\mathbb R of a layer labeling, ⟨f(a0)g(at)⟩β=tr⁡(DfTtDgTN−t)/tr⁡TN\langle f(a_0)\,g(a_t)\rangle_\beta=\operatorname{tr}\big(D_f\,T^t\,D_g\,T^{N-t}\big)/\operatorname{tr}T^N.

Read it as a trip around the stack: measure ff, go up tt slabs, measure gg, and go the remaining N−tN-t slabs back to the start. Let's check it on the 3×33\times3 torus with f=g=wf=g=w, the ring's loop product of a layer: w(a)=a0a1a2w(a)=a_0a_1a_2, the product of its three labels (column indices here). Tall stacks: one eigenvalue wins

First a property we will use constantly: TT is symmetric, T(a,b)=T(b,a)T(a,b)=T(b,a). Swapping the roles of the two layers is the flip of one slab, which maps faces to faces and does not change any cost (it may invert a face product, and the face costs of glossary 4.2 satisfy φ(x−1)=φ(x)\varphi(x^{-1})=\varphi(x)). For the coin model you can see it directly: the face product axsx+1bxsxa_x\,s_{x+1}\,b_x\,s_x doesn't change if aa and bb trade places. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues λ0≥λ1≥⋯\lambda_0\ge\lambda_1\ge\cdots and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

The trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, and the eigenvalues of TNT^N are the NN-th powers of those of TT. So

Z=tr⁡TN=∑nλnN. Z=\operatorname{tr}T^N=\sum_n\lambda_n^N .

Every entry of TT is a sum of positive weights. For such a matrix the Perron–Frobenius theorem (the one behind the stationary distribution of a Markov chain) says that the largest eigenvalue λ0\lambda_0 is positive and strictly larger than |λn||\lambda_n| for every other nn. Dividing by λ0N\lambda_0^N, every other term (λn/λ0)N(\lambda_n/\lambda_0)^N goes to 00. So as NN grows, λ0\lambda_0 takes over: Z≈λ0NZ\approx\lambda_0^N, and 1Nlog⁡Z→log⁡λ0\frac1N\log Z\to\log\lambda_0. In words: in a tall stack every layer contributes the same factor λ0\lambda_0 to the total weight. You can see it converge.

Why TT only sees loop products

Take a reframing hh that acts only on the vertices of layer k+1k+1. It changes the layer labeling bb into some other labeling h⋅bh\cdot b. It also changes the step labels touching layer k+1k+1, but those are exactly the labels ss that TT sums over. Reframing doesn't change any face product's cost (chapter 2), and it only relabels which ss is which in the sum. So the sum is the same:

Fact 5.1 (layer constraint). For every reframing hh that acts only on one layer, T(a,h⋅b)=T(a,b)T(a,h\cdot b)=T(a,b), and likewise T(h⋅a,b)=T(a,b)T(h\cdot a,b)=T(a,b). So T(a,b)T(a,b) depends only on the classes of aa and bb (glossary 5.10).

On a ring the class of a layer labeling is determined by its loop product (chapter 1). For the coin model that means: T(a,b)T(a,b) depends only on whether w(a)=w(b)w(a)=w(b). 5.4 How fast do layers forget? Eigenvalues

Here is the question that will give us the most important number of the chapter: if you know the loop product at layer 0, how much do you know about it at layer tt?

Iterating the walk by hand

The answer is in the powers PtP^t of the transition matrix: (Pt)(w,w′)(P^t)(w,w') is the probability of going from ww to w′w' in tt steps (the warm-up of 5.2 again). For a 2×22\times2 matrix we can compute PtP^t exactly, using two special vectors. Write PP as

P=12(1+r1−r1−r1+r). P=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+r & 1-r\\ 1-r & 1+r\end{pmatrix}.

Multiply it by the vectors u0=(1,1)u_0=(1,1) and u1=(1,−1)u_1=(1,-1):

Pu0=u0,Pu1=ru1. P\,u_0=u_0,\qquad P\,u_1=r\,u_1 .

So u0u_0 and u1u_1 are eigenvectors, with eigenvalues 11 and rr: PP does not mix them, it only scales them. Read as functions of the loop product, u0u_0 is the constant function and u1u_1 is the function ww itself. Any vector is a combination of the two, for example the starting distribution "certainly +1+1":

(1,0)=12u0+12u1. (1,0)=\tfrac12u_0+\tfrac12u_1 .

(A distribution is usually multiplied by PP from the left. Here PP is symmetric, so left and right give the same vector, and Pt(1,0)⊤P^t(1,0)^{\top} is the first column of PtP^t, whose first entry is (Pt)(+1,+1)(P^t)(+1,+1).) Applying PP a total of tt times multiplies the u0u_0 part by 1t1^t and the u1u_1 part by rtr^t:

Pt(1,0)⊤=12u0+12rtu1=(12+12rt,12−12rt). P^t(1,0)^{\top}=\tfrac12u_0+\tfrac12r^tu_1=\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12r^t,\ \tfrac12-\tfrac12r^t\big).

The first entry is the answer:

Pr⁡(wt=+1∣w0=+1)=12+12rt. \Pr(w_t=+1\mid w_0=+1)=\frac12+\frac12\,r^t .

The constant part, 12\frac12, is where the walk ends up when it has forgotten everything. The memory of the start is the second part, and it shrinks by the factor rr at every layer. The eigenvalue rr is the rate of forgetting.

Levels: rates instead of factors

Exponential decay is easier to talk about as a rate. Write r=e−E1r=e^{-E_1}, so that

rt=e−E1t,E1=−log⁡r. r^t=e^{-E_1t},\qquad E_1=-\log r .

E1E_1 is "how much memory is lost per layer". For the coin model, E1=−log⁡(tanh⁡β)W=−Wlog⁡tanh⁡βE_1=-\log(\tanh\beta)^W=-W\log\tanh\beta.

In general we don't normalize TT into a probability matrix first, so we define the rates directly from the eigenvalues of TT. TT is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real. Order them λ0≥λ1≥⋯\lambda_0\ge\lambda_1\ge\cdots. The levels (glossary 5.8) are

En=−log⁡λnλ0≥0. E_n=-\log\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_0}\ \ge0 .

Dividing by λ0\lambda_0 is what normalizing does: λ0\lambda_0 is the overall weight per layer, and it cancels from every probability. For the coin model you can see this exactly. Every row of T̃\tilde T has the same sum, so the constant vector u0u_0 is an eigenvector of T̃\tilde T with that row sum as eigenvalue, and that is λ0\lambda_0 (checked below). Then P=T̃/λ0P=\tilde T/\lambda_0, so the eigenvalues of PP are those of T̃\tilde T divided by λ0\lambda_0: 11 and r=λ1/λ0r=\lambda_1/\lambda_0. So E0=0E_0=0 always, and the gap E1E_1 is the decay rate of the slowest-forgetting memory. A small gap means long memory; a big gap means fast forgetting.

A warning about signs

We took log\log of eigenvalue ratios without worrying. That needs every relevant eigenvalue to be positive. (Zero eigenvalues are harmless: they belong to the erased functions, which we have just set aside.) A negative eigenvalue would make the memory alternate in sign from layer to layer (rtr^t with r<0r<0), and no decay rate could describe it. For our models this never happens. 5.5 Two roads to one number

We computed the gap from the cost function: build TT, take eigenvalues. Now forget the cost. Suppose someone hands you a black box that produces random labelings of the cylinder from some model, and nothing else. Can you get the same number?

Yes: section 5.4 already said how. Memory decays like e−E1te^{-E_1t}, so measure memory. Write wk=w(ak)∈{±1}w_k=w(a_k)\in\lbrace\pm1\rbrace for the loop product of layer kk's ring and define the layer correlation

C(t)=⟨wkwk+t⟩β,C:{0,…,N}→ℝ, C(t)=\big\langle w_k\,w_{k+t}\big\rangle_\beta,\qquad C:\lbrace 0,\dots,N\rbrace\to\mathbb R ,

The prediction, step by step

On a periodic stack two layers tt apart are joined two ways: tt slabs going up and N−tN-t slabs going the rest of the way round. With the matrices of 5.2 the average is

C(t)=tr⁡(DwTtDwTN−t)tr⁡TN, C(t)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\big(D_w\,T^t\,D_w\,T^{N-t}\big)}{\operatorname{tr}T^N},

where DwD_w is the diagonal matrix that multiplies by the loop product (the rule of 5.2 with f=g=wf=g=w). Now compute the trace in a basis of eigenvectors of TT. Recall that the trace of a matrix MM can be computed in any orthonormal basis, as ∑vv⊤Mv\sum_v v^{\top}Mv; so a basis vector with Mv=cvMv=cv contributes exactly cc. For the space of functions on Ω\Omega (2W2^W-dimensional) take:

  • u0u_0, the constant function 11, with Tu0=λ0u0Tu_0=\lambda_0u_0;
  • u1u_1, the function ww itself, with Tu1=λ1u1Tu_1=\lambda_1u_1 (these are the vectors (1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1) of the walk, now read as functions of the whole layer labeling);
  • the remaining 2W−22^W-2 basis vectors: erased functions gg, with Tg=0Tg=0 (5.4).

(Divide each by its length to make them orthonormal; that doesn't change what they contribute.) An erased gg contributes 00, because the first matrix to act on it, TN−tT^{N-t}, kills it (for 0<t<N0<t<N). Multiplying by ww swaps the other two, since w2=1w^2=1: Dwu0=u1D_wu_0=u_1 and Dwu1=u0D_wu_1=u_0. Now follow u0u_0 through M=DwTtDwTN−tM=D_w\,T^t\,D_w\,T^{N-t}, from right to left: TN−tT^{N-t} scales it by λ0N−t\lambda_0^{N-t}, DwD_w turns it into u1u_1, TtT^t scales by λ1t\lambda_1^t, and DwD_w turns it back into u0u_0. 5.6 Flip-positivity

Twice already we needed every eigenvalue of TT to be nonnegative: to take logarithms in 5.4, and for C(t)C(t) to decay instead of oscillating in 5.5. This section explains the property behind that, flip-positivity. It is worth the time: it is also what turns functions on the board into the vector space of a quantum system (5.8), and it is one of the properties the Millennium problem demands of the limit in chapter 6.

Two ways to put a mirror in the stack

The flip θ\theta turns the stack upside down. There are two natural places to put the mirror:

  • through a layer: layer 00 stays where it is, and layer kk goes to layer −k-k;
  • between two layers: the mirror sits in the middle of slab 0, so layers 00 and 11 swap, and layer kk goes to layer 1−k1-k. The step edges of slab 0 are cut in half by the mirror and turned around.

Either way the mirror splits the periodic stack into an upper half and a lower half, and θ\theta swaps them. On a periodic stack the mirror has a second copy halfway round, so we take NN even. Through a layer, the upper half is layers 0,1,…,N/20,1,\dots,N/2 with the edges among them (the two mirror layers 00 and N/2N/2 belong to both halves). Between layers, the upper half is layers 1,…,N/21,\dots,N/2 with the edges among them; the step edges of slabs 00 and N/2N/2, which the mirrors cut, belong to neither half.

The definition

Write EupperE_{\text{upper}} for the edges of the upper half, and let FF be any function of their labels, F:GEupper→ℂF:G^{E_{\text{upper}}}\to\mathbb C. Its mirror image θF=F∘θ\theta F=F\circ\theta is the same function applied to the mirrored labeling, so it depends only on the lower half. Flip-positivity (glossary 5.7) says:

⟨θF¯F⟩β≥0for every function F of the upper half. \big\langle\,\overline{\theta F}\;F\,\big\rangle_\beta\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{for every function }F\text{ of the upper half.}

In words: a quantity measured on the top half and the same quantity measured on its mirror image are never, on average, anti-aligned. For a real FF this says that FF and its mirror image have a nonnegative average product.

This is not automatic. Averages of products of two different functions can be negative, of course. The claim is about a function and its own mirror image, and it is a real restriction on the model.

The single-layer case is linear algebra

Take the simplest upper-half function, a real function f:Ω→ℝf:\Omega\to\mathbb R of one layer: F=f(at)F=f(a_t) (so the complex conjugate does nothing). Its mirror image is ff of the mirrored layer, which is pp slabs away, with p=2tp=2t (mirror through a layer) or p=2t−1p=2t-1 (mirror between layers). By the trace rule of 5.2,

⟨θFF⟩β=tr⁡(DfTpDfTN−p)tr⁡TN. \big\langle\theta F\;F\big\rangle_\beta=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\big(D_f\,T^{p}\,D_f\,T^{N-p}\big)}{\operatorname{tr}T^N}.

(The model is shift-invariant, so only the separation pp between the two layers matters.) Write TT in its orthonormal eigenvectors v0,v1,…v_0,v_1,\dots (5.2): Tp=∑mλmpvmvm⊤T^p=\sum_m\lambda_m^{\,p}\,v_mv_m^{\top} and TN−p=∑nλnN−pvnvn⊤T^{N-p}=\sum_n\lambda_n^{\,N-p}\,v_nv_n^{\top}. Substituting, and using tr⁡(Dfvmvm⊤Dfvnvn⊤)=(vn⊤Dfvm)(vm⊤Dfvn)=(vm⊤Dfvn)2\operatorname{tr}(D_fv_mv_m^{\top}D_fv_nv_n^{\top})=(v_n^{\top}D_fv_m)(v_m^{\top}D_fv_n)=(v_m^{\top}D_fv_n)^2 (DfD_f is diagonal, hence symmetric), the numerator becomes

∑m,nλmpλnN−p(vm⊤Dfvn)2. \sum_{m,n}\lambda_m^{\,p}\,\lambda_n^{\,N-p}\,\big(v_m^{\top}D_fv_n\big)^2 .

Each term is a square times λmpλnN−p\lambda_m^p\lambda_n^{N-p}. Since NN is even, pp and N−pN-p are both even (mirror through a layer) or both odd (mirror between layers). Even powers are nonnegative whatever the signs of the eigenvalues. Odd powers are nonnegative for sure only if no eigenvalue is negative. So:

Fact 5.3 (flip-positivity for one layer). Let TT be symmetric with positive entries, and NN even. For the mirror through a layer, ⟨θFF⟩β≥0\langle\theta F\,F\rangle_\beta\ge0 for every single-layer FF, automatically. For the mirror between layers, ⟨θFF⟩β≥0\langle\theta F\,F\rangle_\beta\ge0 for every single-layer FF if TT has no negative eigenvalue; if TT does have a negative eigenvalue, it fails for some single-layer FF once the stack is tall enough.

Why the failure: on a tall stack the terms with n=0n=0 dominate, and they add up to λ0N−p∑mλmp(vm⊤Dfv0)2\lambda_0^{N-p}\sum_m\lambda_m^{\,p}(v_m^{\top}D_fv_0)^2. By Perron–Frobenius v0v_0 has positive entries, so you can choose f=vm/v0f=v_m/v_0 (entrywise) for an eigenvector vmv_m with λm<0\lambda_m<0. Then Dfv0=vmD_fv_0=v_m, the sum is just λmp<0\lambda_m^{\,p}<0 for odd pp, and the average is negative.

So the between-layers flip is the demanding one, and "TT has nonnegative eigenvalues" is what flip-positivity says about single layers.

Watching it fail

Negative stiffness is the easy way to break it: a model with β<0\beta<0 prefers defects. Take the coin model with W=3W=3 and β=−0.7\beta=-0.7. The favourite slab now has all 3 of its faces as defects, an odd number, and an odd number of defects in a slab flips the ring's loop product (Fact 5.2). So the loop product tends to flip at every layer. Neighbouring layers become anti-aligned.

Use F=w(a1)F=w(a_1), the loop product of layer 1. The mirror between layers 0 and 1 turns it into w(a0)w(a_0), so flip-positivity requires ⟨w(a0)w(a1)⟩β≥0\langle w(a_0)\,w(a_1)\rangle_\beta\ge0. Let's compute it with the trace rule on a stack of N=8N=8 layers, for both signs of β\beta, and for several separations pp.

def flip_overlap(T, f, p, N):
    """<f(a_0) f(a_p)> on a periodic stack of N layers, by the trace rule."""
    D = np.diag(f)
    return np.trace(D @ mp(T, p) @ D @ mp(T, N - p)) / np.trace(mp(T, N))

Why it holds for our models: a sum of squares

For β≥0\beta\ge0 it never fails, and for the coin model we can see exactly why. Write one face's weight, for a face product f=±1f=\pm1. Since eβf=cosh⁡(βf)+sinh⁡(βf)e^{\beta f}=\cosh(\beta f)+\sinh(\beta f), and cosh\cosh is even while sinh\sinh is odd, eβf=cosh⁡β+fsinh⁡βe^{\beta f}=\cosh\beta+f\sinh\beta. So:

e−β(1−f)=e−β(cosh⁡β+fsinh⁡β). e^{-\beta(1-f)}=e^{-\beta}\big(\cosh\beta+f\,\sinh\beta\big).

Multiply this over the WW faces of a slab and expand the product: 2W2^W terms, each picking, for every face, either cosh⁡β\cosh\beta or fsinh⁡βf\sinh\beta. Now sum over the step labels ss. A step label sxs_x appears in two faces, x−1x-1 and xx. If a term picks fsinh⁡βf\sinh\beta in exactly one of them, that term is sxs_x times something not involving sxs_x, and summing over sx=±1s_x=\pm1 gives (+1)+(−1)=0(+1)+(-1)=0: the term dies. If it picks both, sxs_x appears squared, sx2=1s_x^2=1, and it survives. So a term survives only if, for every xx, faces x−1x-1 and xx are picked alike; going around the ring, that means all faces alike. That leaves just two terms: "pick cosh⁡β\cosh\beta everywhere", and "pick fsinh⁡βf\sinh\beta everywhere". In the second, the product of the WW face products axsx+1bxsxa_x\,s_{x+1}\,b_x\,s_x is w(a)w(b)w(a)\,w(b) times ∏xsx2=1\prod_xs_x^2=1. Neither surviving term depends on ss, so each of the 2W2^W choices of ss contributes the same, and

T(a,b)=(2e−β)W(cosh⁡Wβ+sinh⁡Wβw(a)w(b)). T(a,b)=\big(2e^{-\beta}\big)^W\Big(\cosh^W\!\beta\;+\;\sinh^W\!\beta\;w(a)\,w(b)\Big).

In matrix form, with 𝟏\mathbf 1 the all-ones vector and ww the vector of loop products,

T=c0𝟏𝟏⊤+c1ww⊤,c0=(2e−βcosh⁡β)W,c1=(2e−βsinh⁡β)W. T=c_0\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\top}+c_1\,w\,w^{\top},\qquad c_0=(2e^{-\beta}\cosh\beta)^W,\quad c_1=(2e^{-\beta}\sinh\beta)^W .

The vectors 𝟏\mathbf 1 and ww are orthogonal (𝟏⊤w=∑aw(a)=0\mathbf 1^{\top}w=\sum_aw(a)=0, since half the labelings have w=+1w=+1), and each has squared length 2W2^W. So T𝟏=2Wc0𝟏T\mathbf 1=2^Wc_0\,\mathbf 1 and Tw=2Wc1wTw=2^Wc_1\,w, while TT kills every vector orthogonal to both. That explains everything we saw in 5.3 and 5.4 at once: two distinct entries, only 𝟏\mathbf 1 and ww survive, all other eigenvalues are zero, λ0=2Wc0\lambda_0=2^Wc_0, λ1=2Wc1\lambda_1=2^Wc_1, and λ1/λ0=c1/c0=tanh⁡Wβ\lambda_1/\lambda_0=c_1/c_0=\tanh^W\!\beta. And for any vector ff,

f⊤Tf=c0(∑af(a))2+c1(∑aw(a)f(a))2, f^{\top}Tf=c_0\Big(\sum_a f(a)\Big)^2+c_1\Big(\sum_a w(a)f(a)\Big)^2,

a sum of squares with coefficients c0,c1c_0,c_1. When β<0\beta<0 and WW is odd, c1<0c_1<0, and that is the failure we just watched.

What flip-positivity buys: an inner product

Here is the reason flip-positivity is central rather than technical. For two functions F,F′F,F' of the upper half, define

⟨F,F′⟩θ=⟨θF¯F′⟩β∈ℂ. \langle F,F'\rangle_\theta=\big\langle\,\overline{\theta F}\;F'\,\big\rangle_\beta\ \in\mathbb C .

This is linear in F′F' and conjugate-linear in FF. Swapping FF and F′F' conjugates it (apply the symmetry θ\theta inside the average, and use θθ=\theta\theta= identity). And flip-positivity says exactly that ⟨F,F⟩θ≥0\langle F,F\rangle_\theta\ge0. So it is an inner product on functions of the upper half, except that some nonzero functions may have length zero. Throw those away (formally: take the quotient by them), and what is left is a genuine inner-product space, built from nothing but the distribution and the mirror.

For single-layer functions, take the mirror between layers 0 and 1, and F=f(a1)F=f(a_1), F′=g(a1)F'=g(a_1), so θF=f(a0)\theta F=f(a_0). The trace rule gives ⟨F,F′⟩θ=tr⁡(DfTDgTN−1)/tr⁡TN\langle F,F'\rangle_\theta=\operatorname{tr}(D_f\,T\,D_g\,T^{N-1})/\operatorname{tr}T^N. On a tall stack TN−1≈λ0N−1v0v0⊤T^{N-1}\approx\lambda_0^{N-1}v_0v_0^{\top} and tr⁡TN≈λ0N\operatorname{tr}T^N\approx\lambda_0^N (5.2), so this tends to (Dfv0)⊤T(Dgv0)/λ0(D_fv_0)^{\top}T(D_gv_0)/\lambda_0. For the coin model v0=𝟏/2Wv_0=\mathbf 1/\sqrt{2^W} is constant, so the inner product is proportional to f⊤Tgf^{\top}Tg. The functions of length zero are those with f⊤Tf=0f^{\top}Tf=0. For a matrix with no negative eigenvalues that forces Tf=0Tf=0 (expand ff in eigenvectors), so they are exactly the functions TT erases: the non-invariant ones of 5.4. What survives are the functions of the loop product. That is the 2-dimensional space we have been working in all along, now obtained from the flip.

In general, the vector space of a quantum system is constructed exactly this way: functions of the upper half, with the flip inner product, modulo the functions of length zero. The transfer matrix then acts on it as a positive operator. This is the Osterwalder–Schrader construction (named in the dictionary). It is also why flip-positivity is one of the requirements in the Millennium problem (chapter 6): it is an inequality between averages, so it survives limits, and with it the inner product survives too.

Recap. A mirror through a layer or between two layers swaps the upper and lower halves of the stack. Flip-positivity says a function of the upper half and its mirror image never have a negative average product. For single layers it means TT has no negative eigenvalue; it fails for negative stiffness, as sampling confirms. For our models it holds because the weight across the mirror is a sum of squares with nonnegative coefficients. Its payoff is an inner product on functions of the upper half: the vector space of the derived system.

5.7 The circle: free motion on a group

Now let the labels be angles, G=U(1)G=U(1), with face cost φ(θ)=1−cos⁡θ\varphi(\theta)=1-\cos\theta. Fact 5.2 still holds: the ring's loop product w∈U(1)w\in U(1) is a random walk on the circle, and each step adds WW independent angles, each with density proportional to eβcos⁡θe^{\beta\cos\theta}.

A random walk on a circle is diagonalized by the functions χn(w)=einw\chi_n(w)=e^{in w}, n∈ℤn\in\mathbb Z (the characters of U(1)U(1)). One step multiplies χn\chi_n by the average of einθe^{in\theta} over one step. For one face that average is a ratio of Bessel functions, In(β)/I0(β)I_n(\beta)/I_0(\beta), so

λnλ0=(In(β)I0(β))W,En=−Wlog⁡In(β)I0(β). \frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_0}=\Big(\frac{I_n(\beta)}{I_0(\beta)}\Big)^W,\qquad E_n=-W\log\frac{I_n(\beta)}{I_0(\beta)} .

For a stiff model each face angle is small and roughly Gaussian with variance 1/β1/\beta, so one step is roughly Gaussian with variance W/βW/\beta, and λn/λ0≈e−Wn2/(2β)\lambda_n/\lambda_0\approx e^{-Wn^2/(2\beta)}:

En≈W2βn2. E_n\approx\frac{W}{2\beta}\,n^2 .

The levels lie on a parabola. Let's check this against an honest matrix: replace the circle by K=64K=64 equally spaced angles, build the 64×6464\times64 transfer matrix of the walk, and take its eigenvalues.

def circle_levels_matrix(W, beta, K=64):
    p = np.exp(beta * np.cos(2 * np.pi * np.arange(K) / K)); p /= p.sum()   # one face
    step = p.copy()
    for _ in range(W - 1):                                                   # W faces in a slab
        step = np.real(np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(step) * np.fft.fft(p)))
    ev = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(circulant(step)))[::-1][:15]           # the top few levels
    return -np.log(ev / ev[0])

def circle_levels_bessel(W, beta, n):
    return -W * np.log(ive(n, beta) / ive(0, beta))

5.8 Naming what we built

Step back and look at the objects we derived, using nothing but the model and a layering:

  • a vector space: functions of a layer labeling, Ω→ℂ\Omega\to\mathbb C, restricted to the reframing-invariant ones. Section 5.6 showed where it really comes from: functions of the upper half with the flip inner product, after discarding the functions of length zero. For the coin model on a ring it is 2-dimensional; for the circle it is the space of functions on the circle;
  • a symmetric operator with nonnegative eigenvalues on it, the transfer matrix TT, and its logarithm

H=−log⁡T, H=-\log T ,

whose eigenvalues are the levels EnE_n (after subtracting the smallest, −log⁡λ0-\log\lambda_0);

  • the identity tr⁡TN=tr⁡e−NH\operatorname{tr}T^N=\operatorname{tr}e^{-NH} for the total weight.

This pair (vector space, HH) is the derived system of glossary 5.9. Physicists know it under another name. The vector space is a Hilbert space, HH is the Hamiltonian, its eigenvalues are energy levels, and the whole thing is a quantum system. The coin model on a ring derives a two-level system; the circle model derives a rotor. The periodic stack of NN layers corresponds to that quantum system at temperature 1/N1/N; a very tall stack means temperature near zero, where only the lowest levels matter.

The usual textbook order goes the other way round: start from a quantum system, write e−NHe^{-NH} as a product of NN small steps, insert complete sets of states between them, and call the result a path integral. We never assumed a Hilbert space or a Hamiltonian. We had a distribution on labelings, and they came out.

A word about real time. Our "time" direction is the layer index, and correlations decay like e−Ente^{-E_nt}. The quantum system's actual time evolution is e−iHte^{-iHt}: oscillations instead of decay. In principle the two determine each other, since both are built from the same HH. In practice, recovering oscillations from a sum of noisy decaying exponentials is a notoriously unstable inverse problem: tiny errors in C(t)C(t) hide large changes in the oscillating answer. That is why simulations of real-time dynamics are hard, and why people hope quantum computers will help.

Chapter recap

  • A layered board splits into layers; faces live in slabs between consecutive layers. The shift and flip are symmetries of the board and hence of every model on it.
  • A sum over sequences of products is a matrix power. Summing out step-edge labels slab by slab gives the transfer matrix T(a,b)T(a,b), indexed by layer labelings. The normalizer is Z=tr⁡TNZ=\operatorname{tr}T^N, averages are traces with diagonal matrices inserted, and in a tall stack Z≈λ0NZ\approx\lambda_0^N.
  • Layer constraint: TT ignores reframings of a layer, so only invariant functions matter. On a ring those are functions of the loop product ww, which performs a random walk on GG.
  • Eigenvectors of TT are functions that one step only rescales. Memory of a layer decays like (λ1/λ0)t(\lambda_1/\lambda_0)^t, so the levels En=−log⁡(λn/λ0)E_n=-\log(\lambda_n/\lambda_0) are rates of forgetting and the gap E1E_1 is the slowest one. TT erases non-invariant functions (eigenvalue 0). Coin model: E1=−Wlog⁡tanh⁡βE_1=-W\log\tanh\beta.
  • Flip-positivity: a function of the upper half and its mirror image never have a negative average product. For single layers it means TT has no negative eigenvalue; it fails for negative stiffness and holds for our models because the weight across the mirror is a sum of squares (Osterwalder–Seiler). It provides the inner product of the derived system.
  • Two roads: eigenvalues of TT and the decay of correlations measured from samples give the same gap, within sampling error. Only the distribution and the layering are needed.
  • For the circle the levels are ≈W2βn2\approx\frac{W}{2\beta}n^2: a free particle on a circle, derived, not assumed.
  • The pair (invariant functions of a layer, H=−log⁡TH=-\log T) is what physicists call a quantum system.
